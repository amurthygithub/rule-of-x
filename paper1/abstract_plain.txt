Many people judge software companies by the Rule of 40. The rule adds revenue growth and profit margin. A company passes if the sum is 40% or more. The rule counts a point of growth as equal to a point of margin. The rule measures a score, not value. When growth lasts, a point of growth is worth more than a point of margin. Investors' screens often count it as two to three points. Under an assumption we check but do not prove, a value-maximizing company counts it as more. This holds when its revenue multiple is well above three. We use first-reported SEC filings of 284 software-classified companies, 2010 to 2025. The score that only the top 5% reach fell by 12 to 13 points from 2016-19 to 2023-25. It fell because fewer companies grew fast. The fall is significant on operating margins under most tests, not on cash. Then we ask how high the score can go if AI does all the work. AI is not free: computing, controls and errors still cost money. At our central costs and fixed prices, a mature company with few assets stays below a cash score of 100. The only way past 100 is a market that grows more than 32.5% a year. This assumes AI computing costs 6 cents per dollar of revenue. If computing costs 1.2 cents, the market must grow 28% a year. If it costs 60 cents, the market must grow 78% a year. If the company passes half its savings to customers as lower prices, the market must grow 36% a year. Change takes time. Our central case is a scenario, not a forecast. In it, a top company gets 41% of the benefits by 2036. Its lasting score is then 58, or 53 while it still pays for changes in progress. Sampling error alone puts the 58 between 54 and 62. The changes pay back in 7.5 years. Competition limits the gain. If rivals enter freely and sell the same product, the savings go to customers. Only a company with something rivals cannot copy keeps more than a normal return.
