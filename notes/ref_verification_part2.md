## Notes for the authors: claims in the spec that the literature contradicts or that need a different citation

1. **METR capability path (spec section 6).** Cite kwa2025measuring under its current title, "Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Software Tasks" (NeurIPS 2025). The verified doubling time is 207 days (about 7 months; 95% CI 166-240) for 2019-2025 and 143 days (about 4.7 months) for models since 2024. Use a range for T_d, not one number. A faster TH1.1 figure (about 3.5 months) exists only in secondary reports. The horizon is measured on software tasks at 50% success. 80% horizons are 4-6 times shorter, which gives the spec's χ(r̄) an empirical anchor at 80% but none above it. H0 depends on the setting: Anthropic's January 2026 report finds about 3.5 h (API) and 19 h (Claude.ai) for a model at about 2 h on METR.
2. **Gundlach et al. (spec section 6, "5 to 10x a year for frontier models").** The 5-10x figure is the fall in the price of a *fixed level of benchmark performance*. The price of running the *frontier* model rises 3-18x a year. δ_π should therefore apply at fixed task difficulty. An activity that needs the current frontier model can see its unit price rise.
3. **Epoch 9-900x.** Confirmed, but it is per performance threshold, dated March 2025, and Epoch warns that the fastest declines, which are recent, may not persist.
4. **Energy share s_E and fusion (Q5).** Epoch puts energy at about $0.6bn of $8.5bn annual TCO for a 1 GW AI data center (about 7%), with servers at 60%. Even free energy would lower inference cost by only a few percent unless hardware costs also fall. The fusion channel is second order.
5. **ψ_min "by risk class" (spec section 5).** The EU AI Act sets no minimum review share. Article 14 requires a capability to oversee, override and stop. Two-person verification applies only to remote biometric identification. Annex III lists eight use-case areas, not graded risk classes. GDPR Art. 22 (right to human intervention) is a second source. ψ_min should be presented as a modeling assumption.
6. **Team production (spec section 7).** The quadratic pairwise form χ n(n-1)/2 could not be confirmed in Becker and Murphy (whose abstract stresses coordination costs and general knowledge) or in the Brooks description read here. Treat it as the model's assumption, with Becker-Murphy and Brooks as motivation.
7. **O*NET counts (spec section 2).** There are 2,087 DWAs (not "about 2,000"; release 30.2). The current release is 31.0 (August 2026). The 41 GWAs were not confirmed.
8. **R&D capitalization (spec section 5).** US law changed in 2025 (new sec. 174A, domestic R&E expensing restored). The θ vector should reflect this once a primary source is cited.
9. **Cybernetic Teammate (Q8).** It exists and is published (Organization Science 2026, new subtitle). It supports n* falling toward 1, but the evidence is one firm and one task type (product-innovation proposals).
10. **Project risk (spec 14.1).** Do not use the HBR 27% average as the central overrun. The peer-reviewed JMIS study finds a power-law tail (α ≈ 2.3 above twice the estimate), so the engine should draw overruns from a heavy-tailed distribution, not a normal one. "One in six black swans" (overrun of 200% or more) is HBR-level evidence on 1,471 projects. Neither source gives benefit-realization rates, so b_p and π_p remain assumed.
11. **Learning (spec 14.1).** Learning depreciates and transfers poorly across units (Argote et al.; Darr et al.). The experience term in π should decay over time and be specific to team or platform, not cumulative over the whole program.
12. **Decision latency (spec 14.2).** No empirical estimate of approval latency or its cost was found in the peer-reviewed literature. Radner and van Zandt give the theory of processing delay; ℓ_dec and q(L/Φ) are assumptions.
13. **Parallel running and severance (spec 13.2).** No peer-reviewed source on parallel-run duration or duplication premia was found. The post-adoption dip is documented (McAfee; Hitt, Wu & Zhou; Brynjolfsson & Hitt 2003) but not as a parameter range. Severance norms need statutory or survey sources.
14. **Sponsor hazard (spec 15).** Use Kaplan & Minton for CEO h_L: about 0.16 a year (tenure under 6-7 years). No peer-reviewed tenure data for CIOs, CDOs or CAIOs was sought or found; those defaults must be flagged as practitioner data. Jenter & Kanaan show forced turnover is correlated with industry shocks, which supports correlated shock arrivals.
15. **Reorganizations (spec 15).** Peer-reviewed evidence links reorganization type to firm performance (Girod & Whittington) and change to an immediate rise in failure hazard (Amburgey et al.). None was found on reorganizations and in-flight project outcomes, so T_freeze and the hit to π are assumptions.
16. **Abandonment (spec 15).** Myers & Majd (1990), named in the spec, could not be verified; cite Berger, Ofek & Swary (1996) and Trigeorgis (1996) instead.
17. **Ownership presets (spec 16).** Use T_hold ≈ 6 years (median; Kaplan & Strömberg), realized buyout outperformance of about 3% a year over the S&P 500 (Harris et al.), and the covenant effect (Chava & Roberts). Target IRRs, preferred-return hurdles, VC required returns and software exit multiples have no peer-reviewed source; mark them practitioner and assumed. Guo et al. find that much of the buyout return came from multiple expansion rather than operations, which cautions against crediting PE regimes with large operating gains from transformation. Graham et al. is survey evidence.
18. **Bass parameters (spec 17.1, 17.5).** The abstract of Sultan, Farley & Lehmann (1990) reports no mean p or q, and the usual 0.03 and 0.38 were not read at source. Van den Bulte & Stremersch (2004) give verified means of p = 0.027 and q = 0.419 (medians 0.012 and 0.420). Their sample is 52 consumer durables in 28 countries, not B2B software. Fradkin (2025) finds that demand for a new LLM levels off within weeks, much faster than durables diffuse. Flag p and q for AI features as assumed and calibrate them to the firm's own adoption data.
19. **Pricing-model migration (spec 17.2).** The spec says moving from seats to usage or outcomes "protects revenue." The literature finds no dominant tariff. When metering has transaction costs, a fixed fee plus usage always beats pure usage (Sundararajan 2004), so the target should be a hybrid. Flat-rate bias raises profit without raising churn, while pay-per-use bias raises churn (Lambrecht & Skiera 2006), so migration can forfeit the flat-rate premium. Metering also loses to a rival that sells (Balasubramanian et al. 2015). Model the migration gain net of these effects.
20. **Competitive price floor (spec 17.2).** "Competition pushes θ_cap toward the entrant's cost-plus floor" is one limiting case. Bergemann et al. price tokens at marginal cost but extract rent through the committed-spend menu. Korinek & Vipra (2025) argue that scale and scope economies may tip foundation models toward concentration. Fradkin (2025) finds differentiation and multihoming that sustain pricing power. The floor should be a scenario, not the default.
21. **Data as a scarce input ρ (spec 17.2).** Returns to data diminish: longer histories help, more products barely do (Bajari et al. 2019). Data-savvy entrants can overtake data-rich incumbents (Farboodi et al. 2019). The advantage depends on the learning function (Hagiu & Wright 2023) and erodes if data can be traded (Gans 2024). Model ρ from proprietary data with diminishing returns and possible erosion, not as a fixed moat.
22. **Self-cannibalization (spec 17.3 item 7).** Arrow (1962) supports the premise that incumbents gain less from replacing their own profits than entrants do. In Moorthy & Png (1992), staggering introductions is optimal only with commitment to future prices and qualities, when cannibalization is severe and customers are more impatient than the seller. The timing result should state its commitment assumption.
23. **Gordon-Loeb (spec 18.2).** The exact statement is z*(v) < (1/e)vL, about 37% of the expected loss vL. It is proved only for two classes of breach probability functions. Hausken (2006) shows that other breach functions are not capped at 1/e and can give bang-bang investment. Gordon & Loeb also show that optimal investment need not rise with vulnerability. The engine should not impose 37% as a hard cap, and should not assume that s_sec rises monotonically with exposure e_j.
24. **Maintenance shares (spec 18.1, 18.6).** No verified source gives a maintenance share for AI systems. The often-quoted "75-80 percent" in Lientz et al. (1978) is a prior estimate they cite. Their pilot survey found maintenance roughly equal to new development: 48% vs 46%, per Lientz's ONR report. Schach et al. (2003) show that the survey also misstates the mix, with measured corrective work more than double the survey share. Sculley et al. (2015) are qualitative. The coupling claim is consistent with Sculley (entanglement) and Banker et al. (1993, complexity raises cost), but neither gives a functional form. μ_m, T_up and C_reval stay assumed.
25. **Compliance standards (spec 18.2).** The NIST AI RMF is voluntary and not certifiable; ISO/IEC 42001 is a certifiable management-system standard. Treat RMF alignment as a program cost, not a certification cost. OWASP is practitioner guidance (2025 edition, 17 Nov 2024). Its LLM01 (prompt injection) and LLM06 (excessive agency) support the e_j terms for the external input surface and write rights. LLM10 (unbounded consumption) links to the FinOps item in 18.3.
