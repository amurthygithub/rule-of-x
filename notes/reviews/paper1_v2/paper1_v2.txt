                        The Rule of 40 as a Price Line:
           Efficient Frontiers, Economic Profit and the Limits of AI
                                  Automation
                                       Akshaya Bangalore Sreenivasa Murthy*

                                                         October 2026



                                                             Abstract
        The Rule of 40 (revenue growth plus profit margin of at least 40%) is a widely used benchmark for
        software firms. We model the set of attainable growth-margin pairs. Its efficient frontier is concave
        when capacity is installed ahead of the revenue it serves, with an intercept equal to the margin at market
        growth, and Rule-of-40 and Rule-of-X lines are price lines whose tangency with it gives the maximal
        score: the intercept, plus market growth net of the capital it requires, plus a growth surplus that vanishes
        as market share saturates and, within one market, draws on a finite budget of share headroom. In value
        terms the Rule of X is a rule of economic profit plus growth at its net value. Under a standard verification
        hypothesis, a value-maximizing firm weights growth at the marginal value of revenue, which exceeds the
        screening weights of two to three when its revenue multiple does. In SEC filings as first reported for 292
        software firms in 2010-2025, the 95th percentile of the score fell by 11.9 to 12.7 points between 2016-19
        and 2023-25 as fast growers became scarcer, and among the best performers a point of growth came
        with about a fifth of a point less margin; the data do not identify the weight firms act on. Charging the
        full costs of running AI, complete automation at fixed prices keeps a mature asset-light firm’s cash-basis
        score below 100 unless its market grows faster than about 31% a year if compute absorbs 6% of revenue
        (27 to 51% over the credible range of that share); passing savings to customers raises the threshold, and
        a Rule of 1000 can be sustained only briefly. Execution sets the pace: on a central scenario path an
        exceptional firm realizes 41% of the benefits of its automation program by 2036 and scores about 66.
        We also show how acquired growth and interest distort the score. Under free entry, the limit is set by
        what stays scarce, not by what is automated.

most of the work a software company does.
    This paper asks four questions. What is the efficient frontier of growth and profitability, and how does
the Rule of 40 relate to it? What is the theoretical limit of the score: is it a Rule of 100, a Rule of 1000, or
something else? How might that limit evolve over the next two, five and ten years as AI automates more of
the firm? And what conditions must a firm meet to approach it?
    We answer with a model, an empirical frontier and a calibration. In the model (Section 3), a firm grows
faster than its market only by spending, and the returns to spending diminish with the amount spent and
with the share of the market the firm already holds. The efficient frontier of attainable (growth, margin)
pairs is then decreasing and concave, with an intercept equal to the margin the firm earns when it simply
grows with its market. The Rule of 40 and the Rule of X are price lines, or iso-score lines, not frontiers, and
the maximal score is reached where one of them is tangent to the frontier. In closed form it is the margin
intercept, plus market growth net of the capital it requires, plus a growth surplus that vanishes as the firm’s
share approaches its cap. Within one market and at given retention, every point of that surplus is paid for
out of a finite budget of share headroom, so extreme scores are necessarily transient. The model separates
two weights on growth that practice treats as one: the weight investors use to screen firms, which reflects
the duration of the revenue stream and is consistent with the weights of two to three in use, and the weight
a value-maximizing firm acts on. Under a standard verification hypothesis (Proposition 2), the latter is the
marginal value of a dollar of revenue, which exceeds the screening weight when the firm’s revenue multiple
does. In value terms the Rule of X is a rule of economic profit plus growth at the net value of growth, and
the cash Rule of 40 weights growth at one minus capital intensity, so that for capital intensity above one it
penalizes growth whatever its return (Remark 3); Remark S4 lists the results that carry beyond software and
the conditions they need. For a firm whose decision weight falls with share, fast growth gains most from
automating the growth engine and retention and maturity from automating overhead. Under free entry the
long-run score is pinned down by market growth, the cost of capital, a normal return on customer capital and
the rent on whatever input rivals cannot replicate. And the frontier estimated across firms is drawn through
the points firms chose, so it does not identify the trade-off any one firm faces.
    Two extensions bring the costs and the pace of automation into the frontier. First, full automation re-
places labor with compute, fees, an accountability floor and the recurring costs of maintaining, securing and
supervising AI. We give each floor a closed form. The accountability floor is a pass-fail control standard:
the cheapest design that leaves no reasonable possibility of an undetected material misstatement. Control
layers that share one AI model share its failures, so no design built only from such layers passes once their
common-mode failure is more than remote, however many layers it stacks, and neither cheaper inference
nor scale removes that requirement (Proposition 5). Second, a firm reaches its full-cost frontier only through
transitions that fail, roll back and draw on limited capacities to absorb change, decide and review deploy-
ments, and that new leaders, strategies and reorganizations can disrupt. In the frontier attainable by a given
date, risk acts like a cut in capacity: it delays the approach to the static frontier without lowering it, while
partial realization of benefits lowers the level to which the firm converges (Proposition 6 and Proposition 7).

     Combining model and estimates (Section 7), we find that automation alone does not set the limit of the
rule. Inverting the model firm by firm for the best scores of 2023–25 shows that they exceeded their durable
level if little of their selling and marketing expense buys growth; how much of them was transient the data
do not determine. Under full automation, and holding prices fixed, the cash-basis score of a mature firm is
bounded by its after-tax margin net of compute costs, fees, an accountability floor and the recurring costs
of maintaining, securing and supervising AI, plus market growth net of capital needs. If compute absorbs
6% of revenue at full automation at 2025 prices, a scenario value, that bound is 74 to 99 points for market
growth of 5 to 30%, so an asset-light firm exceeds 100 only if its market grows faster than about 31% a year
(22% with favorable parameters). Every threshold is conditional on the scenario compute share: over its
credible range of 1 to 30% of revenue the central threshold runs from 27 to 51%. Along a scenario path of
the compute share it is 36, 33 and 30% in 2028, 2031 and 2036, and if half of the industry’s cost savings pass
to customers it rises to 36% at 2025 prices. A Rule of 1000 is not a steady state: it requires 9 to 20 times
the demand pull of today’s best public software firms, and the share-headroom budget limits how long any
such surplus can last. The pace of approach is limited by execution. Under central execution an exceptional
firm realizes about 41% of the benefits of its automation program by 2036, having embedded about 61% of
it, and under fast execution it embeds all of it by 2032; while it is transforming, the costs of transitions in
flight lower its cash score by about 2.5 to 3.1 points. On the central scenario path, which is not a forecast,
the maximal score of an exceptional asset-light firm is about 56 in 2028, 60 in 2031 and 66 in 2036, and by
2036 the largest swing in a one-at-a-time sensitivity comes from market growth. Under free entry a rent on
a scarce input cannot lift the score above the full-cost ceiling (Proposition 4(d)). The levers that move the
limit are therefore a non-replicable input, exceptional demand pull, capital that earns its keep, pricing that
captures falling unit costs, small accountability and full-cost floors and a fast-growing market; a sustained
Rule of 100 requires them jointly to push the durable score above 100.

stock-based compensation, capitalized computing capacity, gross revenue recognition and run-rate revenue

expense are treated. Its 2025 GAAP score, recomputed from its Form F-1, is 115.9 before interest and 105.0

life of the revenue bought. The company reports material weaknesses in its internal control over financial
reporting, and we use its figures as filed. Acquired growth is why a claim about the frontier is stated only
where it holds both in observed scores and on the organic frontier.
     The paper builds on several literatures (Section 2). The tangency between a growth–profit transforma-
tion curve and a valuation line goes back to Marris (1964), Solow (1971) and Odagiri (1981); our growth
technology is a reduced form of customer capital and market penetration (Arkolakis, 2010; Gourio and
Rudanko, 2014) and resembles the advertising models of Vidale and Wolfe (1957), Nerlove and Arrow
(1962) and Sethi (1973), and recent evidence that firms grow by acquiring customers at roughly constant
markups (Fitzgerald et al., 2024; Argente et al., 2025) supports it. The only peer-reviewed academic study
of the rule we are aware of evaluates it as an investment screen (Lee, 2024). Empirically, we adapt partial-
frontier estimators (Cazals et al., 2002; Aragon et al., 2005) to a frontier on which both outputs are desirable,
and we map the effects of AI automation (Aghion et al., 2019; Acemoglu, 2025; Babina et al., 2024) onto
the parameters that determine a firm’s score. The results we believe are new are the score budget, the firm’s
decision weight on growth and its relation to screening weights, the non-identification of firm frontiers from
chosen points, the accountability floor as a control standard under common-mode failure, and the attainable
and expected frontiers; the rest follows from the functional form or is a special case of a standard model.
A companion paper (Bangalore Sreenivasa Murthy, 2026) derives the model’s parameters from the tasks a
firm automates; this paper does not depend on it.

2    Related Literature
Our analysis connects six bodies of work: theory and evidence on the trade-off between firm growth and
profitability; the valuation of growth, its duration and the practitioner rules that summarize it; frontier esti-


                                                       4
mation; the economics of AI and automation and its effects on firms; markups, rents and concentration; and
recent work on AI-assisted research. For each, we keep the canonical statement and add the work of 2018

2.1   Growth and profitability: theory and evidence
The idea that growth is costly at the margin is old. Penrose (1959) argued that expansion is bounded by
the managerial services available to plan and absorb it, so faster growth raises unit costs. Uzawa (1969)
formalized this “Penrose effect” as a concave relation between investment and the growth it buys, and the
adjustment-cost models of Lucas (1967) and Hayashi (1982) gave it a neoclassical form in which marginal
q governs investment and equals average q under linear homogeneity. The closest antecedents of our model
are Marris (1963, 1964), Solow (1971) and Odagiri (1981), in which the firm’s objective (value, growth, or
a managerial preference over both) selects a point on a frontier trading growth against profitability or valu-
ation. They remain the canonical treatments. Our feasible set is the same kind of object: a transformation
curve between growth and margin, with each Rule-of-X line acting as a price line, so the score-maximizing
point is a tangency. Mueller (1969, 1972) extended the managerial view to conglomerate mergers and to
a life-cycle account in which growth opportunities decline as firms mature. On the financial side, Higgins
(1977) showed that sustainable growth is pinned down by margin, asset turnover, leverage and payout, a link
that our capital-intensity correction and our restatement in terms of economic profit generalize.
     The growth technology also has antecedents in marketing science. Growth bought by spending and
limited by the unserved market resembles the sales-response model of Vidale and Wolfe (1957) and the
goodwill model of Nerlove and Arrow (1962), and our optimal-spend condition, under which spend equals
the spend elasticity times the net score value of the growth it buys, is a Dorfman and Steiner (1954) condition.
Modern firm-level evidence supplies the structure behind that technology. Exporters grow after entry by
building a customer base through marketing and advertising, with flat markups and a customer base that
responds little to past sales, rather than through low introductory prices (Fitzgerald et al., 2024); successful
entrants build market share by reaching more customers, with no markup differences between new and
established markets (Argente et al., 2025); and in a working paper, a firm’s market share is tied mainly
to its number of customers and its markup only to its sales per customer (Afrouzi et al., 2023). Growth
bought with spend at roughly constant margins per customer, separate from the margin intercept, is what
Assumption 2 posits. Empirically, the link between growth and profit, in either direction, is weak and varies
across samples (Coad, 2007; Davidsson et al., 2009; Federico and Capelleras, 2015; Lee, 2014), and firm-
level effects explain more of the variance in both than industry effects do (Goddard et al., 2009), which
cautions against reading industry averages as frontier positions.
     The closest modern relatives of our growth technology are models of customer capital. Reaching ad-
ditional consumers in a market involves rising marginal penetration costs (Arkolakis, 2010); with search
frictions the customer base is a slow-moving state variable that firms build with selling and marketing
spend, which links that spend to investment, markups and Tobin’s q (Gourio and Rudanko, 2014); customer
relationships can be modeled as capital acquired by marketing (Drozd and Nosal, 2012); and new plants
stay small because of demand-side fundamentals: their customer base must be accumulated, and active ac-
cumulation driven by past production dominates passive accumulation (Foster et al., 2016). In the q-theory
of advertising, the shadow price of brand capital governs advertising and brand equity is a sizable share of
firm value (Vitorino, 2014; Belo et al., 2022), and in the optimal-control models of advertising the costate
is the marginal value of a unit of sales or share (Sethi, 1973, 1977; Feichtinger et al., 1994). Measured


                                                       5
short- and long-term advertising elasticities of the sales level are small (Sethuraman et al., 2011), a bracket
on diminishing returns rather than an estimate of our spend elasticity, which applies to excess growth. Our
growth technology, its first-order condition for spend and the reading of the multiplier as the marginal value
of a customer are therefore not new: the growth technology is a customer-capital structure in a normalization
by revenue and market size, and the firm’s weight on growth in Proposition 2 is the marginal q of revenue
of this literature. What we add is the reading of the optimum as the maximizer of a Rule-of-X score at that
weight, the contrast between that weight and an investor’s screening weight, and the result that neither can
be recovered from the cross-section of chosen points.

2.2   Valuing growth: duration and the practitioner rules
In the dividend-discount model of Gordon (1959, 1962) and the valuation framework of Miller and
Modigliani (1961), growth adds value only when the return on new investment exceeds the cost of capi-
tal, so any linear rule that adds growth to margin embeds an implicit exchange rate between the two; our
Remark 3 makes that rate explicit in terms of economic profit, and in steady state it restates the constant-
growth valuation ratio of the managerial and dividend-discount models (Marris, 1963; Gordon, 1962) in the
form of a score. Schwartz and Moon (2000) showed that high valuations of loss-making internet firms can be
rationalized by uncertain, mean-reverting revenue growth, and McCarthy et al. (2017) valued subscription
businesses from disclosed customer metrics. The sensitivity of value to growth in these models is a duration
effect in the sense of Macaulay (1938); our derivation of the screening weight as a duration connects to the
implied equity duration of Dechow et al. (2004). Cash flows of longer duration earn lower expected returns:
long-duration stocks earn about 1.1% a month less than short-duration stocks (Weber, 2018), and the value,
profitability and investment premia reflect a premium on near-term cash flows (Gormsen and Lazarus, 2023).
If the discount rate falls with duration, the firm’s own weight on growth is higher than our constant-rate cal-
culation gives, which widens its gap with screening weights. Short-termism points the same way: firms that
just meet analyst forecasts have lower R&D growth and subsequent innovation, and in an estimated growth
model short-termism slows growth (Terry, 2023), as a firm acting on a screening weight below its marginal
value of revenue would. The saturation term in our model resembles Bass-type diffusion (Bass, 1969).
     The Rule of 40 itself is a practitioner construct. Feld (2015) popularized it after hearing it from a late-
stage investor: revenue growth plus profit margin should reach 40%. Consulting studies find that it is rarely
met and is associated with valuation premia. Depeyrot and Heap (2018) report that only 16% of 86 public
software firms beat it in every year from 2013 to 2017 and that firms above the line traded at roughly double
the EV/revenue multiple; Roche and Tandon (2021) reach similar conclusions for more than 200 software
firms over 2011 to 2021. Later work raised or reweighted the threshold. Bosche et al. (2022) asked whether
60 is the new 40; Deeter and Bondy (2024) proposed the Rule of X and recommend multiplying growth by
about 2 for private firms and 2 to 3 for public firms before adding free cash flow margin; and Vista Equity
Partners (2026) describe a Rule of 60 as an emerging aspiration for AI-enabled software firms. In the other
direction, Lipman et al. (2026) argue that inference adds variable cost to software margins and that firms
reinvesting in AI may need to accept a Rule of 30 for a time. The only peer-reviewed academic study of
the rule we located is Lee (2024), who evaluates it as an investment screen on 1,756 SaaS firms over 2003
to 2022 and proposes a “Rule of 65.” We found no modern peer-reviewed study of the growth-profitability
frontier of software firms beyond it.
     Scores also depend on which margin is used. Bradshaw and Sloan (2002) document the widening gap
between GAAP and Street earnings, Barth et al. (2012) study why pro forma and Street earnings continued

to exclude stock-based compensation after SFAS 123R, and Mohanram et al. (2020) find that analysts who
exclude it issue optimistically biased price targets. This motivates our explicit treatment of stock-based

spend and maintenance has a market-based counterpart: exit prices imply the long-lived share of SG&A and

2.3   Frontier estimation
Our empirical frontier draws on two traditions. Stochastic frontier analysis, introduced by Aigner et al.
(1977) and Meeusen and van den Broeck (1977) and surveyed by Kumbhakar and Lovell (2000), separates
noise from one-sided inefficiency under parametric assumptions. Deterministic envelopment, beginning
with the data envelopment analysis of Charnes et al. (1978), avoids functional-form assumptions but is
sensitive to outliers. Partial frontiers address that sensitivity: the order-m frontier of Cazals et al. (2002)
and the conditional-quantile frontier of Aragon et al. (2005), both treated in Daraio and Simar (2007), are
robust to extreme observations. Upper-quantile regression (Koenker and Bassett, 1978) offers a parametric
counterpart. We have not found prior applications of these estimators to the growth-margin plane of software
firms.

2.4   AI, automation and firms
Our reduced-form mapping of AI effects is informed by task-based models of automation, in which au-
tomation displaces labor from tasks while new tasks reinstate it (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2018); we do not
model reinstatement. Aghion et al. (2019) show that automation can coexist with balanced growth because,
as in Baumol (1967), sectors that resist automation come to dominate expenditure and constrain aggregate
growth. Trammell and Korinek (2023), Davidson (2021) and Erdil and Besiroglu (2023) examine conditions
under which AI could produce much faster growth, Erdil et al. (2025) build an integrated assessment model
of AI automation, and Korinek and Suh (2024) set out scenarios for the path to general AI, the precedent
for presenting our horizons as scenarios. Acemoglu (2025) argues from task exposure and cost-savings es-
timates that aggregate productivity effects over the next decade are likely to be modest. Technical exposure
also overstates what is worth automating: in a working paper on computer vision, only 23% of the wages
paid for exposed tasks would be economical to automate at current costs (Svanberg et al., 2024).
    Firm-level evidence bears on the channels in Table 1. Brynjolfsson et al. (2025b) document productivity

2.5   Markups, rents and concentration
Work on markups and intangibles explains why high scores are possible at all: De Loecker et al. (2020) doc-
ument rising markups, Haskel and Westlake (2017) emphasize the scalability of intangible assets, Crouzet
et al. (2022) and Corrado et al. (2022) set out how non-rival, partly excludable intangibles scale and how
to measure them, and Peters and Taylor (2017) and Crouzet and Eberly (2023) show how intangible capital
and rents alter the relation between q and investment. Our free-entry result formalizes the implication that,
where entry is free, AI-driven cost reductions raise scores durably only if they accrue to scarce inputs. Its
equilibrium is the homogeneous-product case of free entry with fixed costs and an integer number of entrants
(Mankiw and Whinston, 1986); with differentiated products the benchmark would be monopolistic compe-
tition (Dixit and Stiglitz, 1977), and switching costs and network effects are one source of the non-replicable
input that sustains a rent (Farrell and Klemperer, 2007). Rising markups have gone with the rise of super-
star firms (Autor et al., 2020). Which inputs stay scarce is itself a question of modern theory. Data-driven


                                                      8
markets tend to tip when more user data lower the cost of quality (Prüfer and Schottmüller, 2021), and the
largest firms are valued mainly for their data (Farboodi and Veldkamp, 2023); a data rent is durable when
the market tips and fades when it does not (see also Hagiu and Wright, 2023). Because data are nonrival,
such a rent also requires that the data be excluded from rivals (Jones and Tonetti, 2020), and competition in
markets for training data, input data and predictions determines how much of it a firm keeps (Gans, 2026).
At the level of the AI supply chain, the amortized cost of training the most compute-intensive models has
grown about 2.4 times a year since 2016 (Cottier et al., 2024), a preprint: a rising fixed cost of the kind that
concentrates supply, as do economies of scale and scope in foundation models (Korinek and Vipra, 2025),
and with it the exposure to a single provider that our common-mode failure term prices. That term has a long
history in reliability engineering, where common-cause failures are the dependent failures that redundancy
does not remove (Mosleh et al., 1989, 1998) and the beta-factor model is the most common form (Hokstad
and Rausand, 2008); in software, versions developed independently still fail together (Eckhardt and Lee,
1985; Littlewood and Miller, 1989; Knight and Leveson, 1986). Our accountability floor applies the term to
stacked reviewers of AI output: layers built the same way do not deliver independence.

2.6   AI-assisted research
Automated research systems are advancing quickly (Lu et al., 2024; Si et al., 2025; Gottweis et al., 2026),
and Korinek (2023) catalogs uses of generative AI in economics. arXiv requires authors to report significant
use of generative AI, states that such tools should not be listed as authors, and holds authors responsible for

2.7   Our contribution
Relative to this literature, the growth technology and its first-order condition are standard. We read the Rule
of 40 and the Rule of X as price lines on a frontier of attainable growth and margin, distinguish the firm’s
own weight on growth (the marginal value of revenue) from the investor’s screening weight and show that
the cross-section of chosen points identifies neither, derive a score budget that bounds how long extreme
scores can last, charge the full costs of running AI against the ceiling, including an accountability floor
whose common-mode part does not vanish under automation, and define the frontier attainable by a given
date when transitions are risky and capacity is limited. Empirically, we estimate the theory’s quantile frontier
on a panel of software firms with every item as first reported, under four definitions of the sample, and state a
claim only where it holds in all of them. Unlike Lee (2024), who evaluates the rule as an investment screen,
we model the frontier it is applied to, and its limits.


3     A Model of the Growth–Profitability Frontier
This section builds a model in which a firm spends part of its revenue on growth, so that faster revenue
growth costs current free cash flow margin. The model gives an efficient frontier between growth and mar-
gin and a closed form for the best attainable Rule-of-X score. It identifies three limits on that score: market
saturation, the finite headroom of market share, and competitive replication. It shows that, under a stated
verification hypothesis, a value-maximizing firm maximizes a Rule-of-X score at every date, with a weight
equal to the marginal value of a dollar of revenue, and it explains how that weight relates to the weights
investors use to screen firms. We then extend the model to heterogeneous firms, which relates it to the cross-
sectional evidence of Section 5, and map the effects of AI automation onto its parameters. Finally, we charge

                                                       9
the full cost of running AI against the ceiling of the score, derive the frontier a firm can reach by a given
horizon when transitions are risky and organizational capacity is limited, and restate the objective in terms
of economic profit for industries other than software. Supporting results are collected in Appendix A and
in Section S1 of the Supplementary Appendix, and all proofs are in Section S3 of the Supplementary Ap-
pendix. Results that follow directly from the functional form of the growth technology are stated as lemmas
and remarks, and the free-entry result is a special case of a standard model. The results we believe are new
are the score budget (Proposition 1), the firm’s decision weight on growth and its relation to the screening
weights of investors (Proposition 2 and Lemma 5), non-identification of firm frontiers from chosen points
and within-firm co-movement (Propositions 3 and S3), the accountability floor as a control standard under
common-mode failure (Proposition 5), and the attainable and expected frontiers (Propositions 6 and 7). The
script code/verify_theory.py checks the closed forms, first-order conditions and envelope derivatives
symbolically, checks the concavity, limit, inequality and probability claims numerically on random param-
eter draws or by simulation, and labels as arithmetic the checks that verify only the numbers of an example.

3.1   Setup
We describe a firm at a point in time. Revenue is S, the addressable market is M, and market share is s = S/M.
Growth rates are instantaneous (continuously compounded) rates, so revenue growth equals market growth
plus share growth. The two conventions for growth rates differ materially only at growth rates of the order
of 100% or more; Section S2.1 of the Supplementary Appendix states which results use which. Margins and
spending are expressed as fractions of current revenue.

means that spend can be financed externally without limit. Revenue grows at

  (b) At g = G the frontier takes the value m∗ (G) = m0 − kG, and its right derivative there is −k.

rate is only −k: the first unit of excess growth costs almost no spend, only the capital needed to serve it. The
rate of steepening is governed by β = 1/α. Points strictly below the frontier waste margin.

3.7   Heterogeneous firms: the industry frontier as an upper envelope
Empirical frontiers are drawn across firms. Section 5 estimates the slope of the χ-quantile frontier Ψχ of
Definition 2, the χ-quantile of the margin among firm-years growing at least g, at −0.22 on the GAAP
basis and −0.16 after stock-based compensation, and finds a within-firm slope near zero. That section also
reports the local conditional quantile of the margin given growth near g. It is a different, descriptive object:
it conditions on growth near g rather than at least g, and the results below speak to Ψχ , not to it. This
subsection shows what each fact can and cannot reveal. The cross-sectional slope is consistent with two

different structures. One is a common frontier, on which firms that act on different weights choose different

selection by quality, in which firms with more growth opportunity also have higher margin intercepts (part
(ii) below). The cross-section cannot tell these apart (part (iv)). If firms act on weights above the steepest
slope of a common frontier, as they would if their decision weights were close to their revenue multiples
(Proposition 2), only the second structure fits. The within-firm slope requires more (part (v)): anticipated
shocks to opportunity would make it negative for every weight above k, so unanticipated demand shocks or
costs committed in advance must offset them.

3.8   Replication and the competitive limit

the product, entry competes margins down. We formalize this with Cournot competition and free entry in a
growing market. The result is a homogeneous-product, integer-entry case of the free-entry model of Mankiw
and Whinston (1986); we make no welfare statement, and what we add is the limit of the score as replication
becomes free and the role of a scarce input and of customer capital in that limit.

Intuition. In a zero-economic-profit steady state, the operating margin covers the cost of capital and of cus-
tomer capital plus a markup L that is the growth-adjusted return on the sunk replication cost. As automation
drives the replication cost to zero, the markup vanishes and the price falls to full unit cost, so automation’s
cost savings pass to customers. What remains is (r − G)(k + ka ), a normal return on physical capital and
on the capitalized cost of acquiring customers, plus the rent ρ on whatever input stays scarce: proprietary
data, distribution, regulatory standing, frontier compute, or switching costs that lock customers in (Farrell
and Klemperer, 2007). Customer capital is not a rent, and automating the product does not remove it unless
it also makes customers cheaper to acquire. Under free entry and replication of a homogeneous product,
then, the limit of the Rule of 40 is set by normal returns on capital and customer capital and by rents on
what stays scarce, not by how much can be automated. At given output prices the conclusion is the opposite:
the saturated score rises by 1 − τ points for every point of human cost or overhead removed, up to the full-
automation ceiling of Corollary 4. With G = 0.05, r = 0.10, k = 0.5 and no customer capital, a competitive
firm without a scarce input scores XG + 0.025, for example 7.5 points for X = 1; a firm that spends 10%
of revenue acquiring the customers that market growth brings has ka = 0.10/0.05 = 2 and earns a further
(r − G)ka = 10 points. Part (d) puts the limit on the cost basis of the ceiling: a rent can at most close the gap
between the competitive score and the full-cost ceiling, and it cannot lift the score above that ceiling.

3.10    The attainable frontier over time
The frontier of Lemma 1 and the ceiling are static: they describe where a firm can be once a transformation
is complete. Getting there takes time. Transitions run in parallel with legacy systems, fail and roll back, and
draw on limited organizational capacities: how many affected employees the firm can absorb, how many


                                                        28
initiatives its leaders can decide and govern at once, how many deployments its security team can review,
and how deep a score dip its owners tolerate. Leaders, strategies and structures also change before the work
is done. This subsection takes these capacities and hazards as primitives and turns them into a frontier that
is attainable by a given horizon. The companion paper (Bangalore Sreenivasa Murthy, 2026) derives each
capacity, the success probability of a transition and the path of the dip from a task-level model of the firm;
nothing below depends on those derivations.

Intuition. The attainable frontier moves out in steps, one wave at a time. The upper bound of Proposition 6(d)
is concave in the number of waves, because the processes with the most score per unit of the binding capacity
go first; the attainable score itself need not be concave, because the packing is in whole processes and uses
several resources. It reaches the static frontier only once the binding capacity has had time to work through
the whole program. Risk changes this picture in two ways. Failures, rollbacks and disruptions by new
leaders or new strategies waste capacity: each embedded process costs 1/pemb attempts on average. Freezes
dilate time, the embedding lag included, by the factor 1 + ν R Tfrz . For programs with many waves these
risks act approximately like a cut in the program’s pace by the factor pemb /(1 + ν R Tfrz ), and they delay the
approach to the static frontier without lowering it. Partial realization of benefits, by contrast, lowers the level
to which the firm converges, because a process’s realized share does not change when it is migrated again.
That is an assumption, and it matters: if each attempt drew a fresh share and reverting were free, a firm could
revert low draws and try again, and in the long run it would approach the level set by the best possible draw
rather than by the mean. Leadership and strategy hazards matter only while work is in flight, so they bear

3.11   Beyond software: economic profit and ROIC
The Rule of 40 is a software convention; the model is not. This subsection restates the objective in value
terms that apply to any industry. The restatement is an exact change of variables, so it does not widen the
model’s domain: that is set by Assumptions 1–4. For a point (g, m) of a firm with capital intensity k and cost
of capital r, define the economic margin e := m + k(g − r): operating profit less the charge for the invested
capital kS, per dollar of revenue, so that eS is economic profit, ROIC net of the cost of capital times invested
capital. For k > 0, define the return on invested capital ROIC := (m + kg)/k.
Remark 3 (Value-based objectives). Under Assumptions 1–4:
  (a) (One frontier, three coordinates.) The economic-margin frontier is e∗ (g) = m∗ (g) + k(g − r) = m0 −
      rk − κ(g − G)β . It is concave, has slope zero at G and is strictly decreasing beyond. For k > 0 the
      ROIC frontier is [m0 − κ(g − G)β ]/k. The same spend levels are efficient in all three coordinates.
  (b) (The Rule of X as economic profit plus growth.) For every (g, m), RX (g, m) = (X − k)g + e + rk =
      k [ROIC + (X/k − 1)g]. Maximizing the Rule of X is therefore the same as maximizing the economic
      margin plus growth at the weight X − k, the net value of growth, or ROIC plus growth at the weight
      qK − 1 with qK := X/k. When X is the firm’s own weight X dyn of Proposition 2, qK is the marginal
      value of a dollar of invested capital; for another weight, such as the Rule of 40’s X = 1, it is a label.
      Growth is bought if and only if qK > 1, as in Lemma 2(b) and (c).
  (c) (Value.) At a constant growth rate g < r and constant margins, V /S = m/(r − g) = k + e/(r − g)
      and V /(kS) = (ROIC − g)/(r − g) = 1 + (ROIC − r)/(r − g), and the FCF yield plus growth equals
      r. Iso-value lines in (g, e) pass through (r, 0), so a family of parallel iso-score lines contains exactly
      one iso-value line. If v0 > k, then at the small-share point (g◦ , m◦ ) of Proposition 2(d) the iso-value
      line of slope −(v0 − k) is tangent to the economic-margin frontier: −e∗′ (g◦ ) = v0 − k and e∗ (g◦ ) =
      (v0 − k)(r − g◦ ).
  (d) (The Rule of 40 as a special case.) With k = 0, e = m, so the Rule of X in (g, m) is the economic-profit
      criterion at the firm’s own weight. With k > 0, the cash Rule of 40 is R1 = (1 − k)g + e + rk. It weights
      growth at 1−k, not X −k: for k > 1 it penalizes growth whatever its return, for k = 1 it ignores growth,
      and for 0 < k < 1 it gives growth a positive weight that is below X − k whenever X > 1. At a given
      economic margin and growth rate it changes with capital intensity at the rate r − g.
    Part (c) is the steady-state valuation ratio of constant-growth models in the tradition of the managerial
firm (Marris, 1963, 1964) and of the dividend-growth model (Gordon, 1962); parts (a), (b) and (d) are
identities. The remark therefore restates the model; it adds no result. What carries over to other industries is
the set of results that need only a concave growth technology, which Remark S4 lists, under three conditions
that need checking outside software. Growth must be bought largely with expensed spend at diminishing
returns, as the evidence on customer acquisition suggests for firms in general (Fitzgerald et al., 2024; Argente
et al., 2025). Capital must scale with revenue, which fails where capacity is lumpy (Remark S1(a) treats
fixed costs). Headroom must shrink with share, which fails under network effects (Remark S1(d)). What
is specific to software is the conventions and the numbers: the pass mark of 40 and the screening weights

                                                      32
of 2 to 3; the empirical results of Section 5 and the scenario range of α; the cost structure behind Table 4
(compute, fees and the accountability floor), and with it the ceiling thresholds; and the horizon ranges of
Section 7. For a capital-intensive firm the right screen is the economic margin plus growth at the net weight
X − k. At k = 1.5 and 15% growth with r = 0.10, for example, the cash Rule of 40 scores a firm 7.5 points
below an asset-light firm with the same economic margin and growth, and each further point of growth
lowers its score by half a point.


4     Data and Measurement
4.1    Sample
We build a firm-year panel from the XBRL financial data that companies file with the U.S. Securities and
Exchange Commission (U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, 2026b). The universe is every EDGAR
registrant whose standard industrial classification (SIC) code is 7370 to 7374 (computer programming, data
processing and related services). The EDGAR company index by SIC code lists inactive as well as active
registrants, so firms later acquired or delisted remain in the universe and the sample does not condition on
survival. For the 662 registrants in this universe that ever report at least $50 million of revenue, we retrieve
every fact they filed in annual reports (forms 10-K, 20-F and 40-F, including amendments) for 35 US-GAAP
elements.
     Every item for a fiscal year is taken as first reported: from the original annual filing for that year, the
earliest-filed annual report that contains a revenue fact for the fiscal year. Growth is computed within that
same filing, which reports the prior year alongside the current one. This matters. Later filings restate prior
years, most often when a divested business is reclassified as discontinued operations, so mixing vintages
produces spurious growth; for example, the restated continuing-operations revenue of AppLovin for 2023
is 35% below its originally reported 2022 total, while growth as first reported was +17%. Revenue is the
largest revenue element reported for the year in that filing, which prevents the selection of a component.
Items that the original filing does not report are taken from the earliest later filing that does; operating
income, operating cash flow, capital expenditure and stock-based compensation come from the original
filing in 96.2 to 99.8% of the sample firm-years. Fiscal years follow the Compustat convention: a year
ending in January to May is assigned to the previous calendar year. We drop seven duplicate registrants
(for example, a holding company and its operating subsidiary filing identical financial statements) and the
overlapping years of three predecessor–successor pairs.
     The sample is prepackaged software (SIC 7372), the industry to which the Rule of 40 is usually applied,
in fiscal years 2010 to 2025, with prior-year revenue of at least $100 million and growth between −90% and
500%; margins outside −300% to 100% are set to missing. In 88% of firm-years the registrant has a U.S.
business address; the rest are mostly foreign private issuers reporting under US GAAP, whose dollar growth
includes currency movements.

4.2    Sample rules
The review of the first version found firm-years that a frontier of software firms should not contain. We
therefore define five rules, chosen before any estimate on the resulting sample was computed (Appendix B):
three exclude firm-years, one flags them, and one can be checked only on the firms the review panels named.
      • R1, first reported. A firm-year is excluded when its values come only from a filing whose own


                                                      33
      reporting period ends later, so that the year exists only as a comparative in a later report. This removes
      140 firm-years: 98 years before a registrant’s first annual report (comparatives in the first report after
      an initial public offering, spin-off or merger with a special-purpose acquisition company), 40 fiscal-
      2010 years before a registrant’s first XBRL annual report and 2 years whose own report carries no
      revenue fact. Their median growth is 24.4%, against 15.4% for the other firm-years. The fiscal-2010
      years of registrants that were already public were reported in real time, but the local data cannot
      separate them from pre-listing years, so all are excluded and added back in a sensitivity.
    • R2, principal business. A firm whose principal business is not software should be excluded, but
      classifying every registrant needs its business description, which we have only for the 16 firms that
      the review panels named (120 firm-years). A keyword classifier, run on those descriptions as defined
      before they were read, classes 4 of them as not software: Activision Blizzard, Bally Technologies,
      Electronic Arts and PLAYSTUDIOS (38 firm-years). It misses game publishers and an AI-cloud
      provider that the panels named, THQ, Take-Two, Roblox, Unity and CoreWeave, so it is a partial
      check. Two sensitivities drop the firms it classes as not software and all 16 named firms; removing
      firms from the main sample without the rule would be a decision taken firm by firm.
    • R3, captive revenue. A firm-year is excluded when related parties provide at least 50% of revenue,
      measured by the XBRL related-party revenue elements and, where it was read, by the filing text. In
      the XBRL data 74 firm-years report related-party revenue, and none reaches the threshold (the largest
      share is 26.9%, SINA in 2015). The filing text excludes 1 firm-year: Exzeo Group in 2025, whose
      annual report states that substantially all of its revenue came from customers affiliated with HCI. The
      XBRL element used most often was retired for recent years, so a captive filer since 2024 is caught
      only where its filing text was read.
    • R4, acquisitions. The acquisition flag of Section 4.4 is extended in two ways: when the prior year-end
      goodwill is missing, the increase in goodwill is measured from the latest earlier year-end; and a cheap
      acquisition is flagged when cash paid or the goodwill increase is at least 10% of prior-year revenue
      and growth accelerates, relative to the year’s median, by at least that amount, so that the acquisition
      could explain the acceleration at a price of at most one times revenue. On the earlier sample the flag
      rises from 469 to 485 firm-years. The rule was chosen among five variants after listing the firm-years
      each would flag and before any estimate on the new sample. A third criterion flags a year in which
      acquired businesses contributed at least 10% of revenue since their acquisition dates (ASC 805-10-
      50-2(h)); it reaches 2 core firm-years, both already flagged, so it adds 0 flags. Its coverage is thin,
      because the element is usually tagged on a business-combination axis that the SEC’s frames omit.
    • R5, listed equity. A filer without listed equity, such as a firm whose public debt keeps it filing after a
      take-private, is flagged rather than excluded. A 10-K firm-year is flagged when its cover page reports
      no public float outside the year of an initial public offering or spin-off: 26 firm-years of 7 firms,
      among them Medical Information Technology (Meditech), SunGard, Infor and Epicor. The flagged
      firm-years stay in the sample and a sensitivity drops them. Foreign filers are not tested, because their
      cover pages report shares outstanding rather than public float.
The resulting sample contains 1,788 firm-years for 292 firms (1,929 and 293 before the rules): 465 in 2010–
15, 418 in 2016–19, 458 in 2020–22 and 447 in 2023–25. Table S2 of the Supplementary Appendix reports
every headline statistic with the excluded fiscal-2010 years added back, without the firm-years flagged under
R5 and without the firms named under R2.

Revenue growth is git = Sit /Si,t−1 − 1. We study four margin definitions, each scaled by revenue:
   1. GAAP operating margin, mop : operating income.
   2. Adjusted operating margin, madj : operating income plus SBC, close to the adjusted margins behind
      company-reported scores such as Palantir’s (Palantir Technologies Inc., 2026).
   3. Free-cash-flow margin, mfcf : operating cash flow (from continuing operations where reported) minus
      capital expenditure and capitalized software. It is missing when capital expenditure is not reported
      (2.1% of firm-years) rather than set to zero.
   4. Free-cash-flow margin after SBC, mfcf−sbc : free cash flow minus SBC, which treats equity compensa-
      tion as the economic cost it is and corresponds to the free-cash-flow margin of the model.
The Rule-of-40 score is R = g + m under each definition. US GAAP classifies interest paid and income
taxes paid as operating cash flows (ASC 230-10-45-17(c) and (d)), so the two cash-flow scores are after
interest paid and cash taxes, while the GAAP operating margin is before both. Capital intensity is capital
expenditure plus capitalized software over revenue. Following the model, we treat selling and marketing
expense as the observable part of discretionary growth spend.

4.4     Acquisitions and discontinued operations
Purchased growth is not the growth the model describes. We flag a firm-year as acquisition-affected when
cash paid for acquisitions or the increase in goodwill over the fiscal year is at least 25% of prior-year
revenue, or when reported pro forma revenue exceeds actual revenue by at least 10%; because acquired
revenue arrives mostly in the following year, we flag the following year as well, matching years by fiscal
year rather than by row. With the extensions of rule R4, the flag covers 26% of core firm-years and 42% of
those with growth above 50%. Acquisitions paid in stock and acquisitions whose only trace is the acquiree’s
revenue remain undetected. Discontinued operations are reported in 6% of core firm-years.

        differ by more than one point, and 9 fail other checks of the disclosure. E2 contains 1,378 firm-years

      • Imputation bounds. Because E2 drops most acquisition-affected years, it is not clear whether a differ-

4.6   Why the measurement basis matters
The four definitions produce materially different scores for the same firm-year. In the sample, 14.3% of
firm-years clear 40 on the GAAP basis and 15.9% after deducting SBC from free cash flow, against 26.6%
on the adjusted basis and 30.5% on free cash flow before SBC. The bridge between the bases is large even
at the median. In 2023–25 the median score is 12.0 on the GAAP basis, 26.4 with SBC added back (median
SBC is 12.3% of revenue), 29.2 on free cash flow before SBC and 15.3 after SBC, and the shares clearing
40 are 9.7%, 24.8%, 29.0% and 9.2%. Adding back the interest and cash taxes that the cash score deducts
raises its 95th percentile by 4.8 to 5.2 points in both 2016–19 and 2023–25 and leaves the decline between
them in place (−11.0 points against −11.4 on the same firm-years); adding back interest alone moves the
95th percentile by at most 0.6 points (Table S6 of the Supplementary Appendix). Interest and taxes paid
come from the SEC’s frames, which keep the latest filed value, so unlike the rest of the panel they are not
first reported. Capital spending can change a score by more than 100 points. CoreWeave, which builds and
operates AI computing capacity and which the review panels classified as compute infrastructure rather than
software, grew 168% in fiscal 2025 at a GAAP operating margin of about zero, for a GAAP score of 167;
its free-cash-flow margin after SBC was −154%, for a cash-basis score of 14. Company-reported scores
such as Palantir’s, which combine quarterly year-over-year growth with an adjusted quarterly margin, are
not comparable with any of the four annual measures. We report the GAAP and free-cash-flow-after-SBC
bases throughout and treat the latter as the economically relevant one.

5.2   The top of the distribution fell as fast growers became scarcer
This subsection describes observed scores (E1). The median GAAP score fell from 23.2 in 2010–15 to 12.0
in 2023–25, and the share of firm-years clearing 40 from 17.2% to 9.7%; after deducting SBC from free
cash flow the median fell from 24.0 to 15.3 and the pass rate from 17.0% to 9.2%.
     The top of the distribution fell as well. Between 2016–19 and 2023–25 the 95th percentile of the GAAP
score fell from 55.9 to 43.9, by 11.9 points (90% interval 6.4 to 20.3), and after SBC from 57.3 to 44.6, by
12.7 points (4.2 to 21.6). The decline holds in all four views: on the GAAP basis it is 11.4 points under E2
and 7.8 and 8.3 under the imputation bounds. Against the family of headline statistics, the Holm-adjusted
p-value of the GAAP decline is 0.054 to 0.208 across interval types (Romano–Wolf 0.097), so it survives at
a familywise level of 10% but not 5%. The yearly series (Table S5) shows that the decline is not a steady
trend. The GAAP 95th percentile reached its low of 41.5 in 2024 and recovered to 51.4 in 2025. Measured
from 2016–19 to 2023–24 the change is −13.7 points (−23.0 to −8.4), and to 2025 alone −4.5 (−17.7 to
+8.7).
     The decline reflects a change in composition: fast growers became scarcer. Only 16 firm-years grew
at least 40% in 2023–25, against 64 in 2016–19, and 124 grew at least 20%, against 191. A reweighting
decomposition over ten growth bins, in the manner of DiNardo et al. (1996), separates the two sources.

change to composition (−17.2 to −2.7) and −4.1 to changes within growth bins (−9.7 to +2.8); reweighting
in the other direction gives −7.2 and −4.7. On the cash basis the composition effect is −8.2 and −16.8 in
the two directions, and the within-bin change −4.6 and +4.0. Reweighting to the 2016–19 mix of firm sizes
instead leaves the decline in place (−13.2 points on the GAAP basis), so it is the growth mix, not the size
mix, that changed.

5.3   At the top, a point of growth costs well under a point of margin
The primary slope is that of Ψ0.90 over growth of 0 to 60%. It is −0.22 (standard error 0.11; 90% interval
−0.42 to −0.05) on the GAAP basis and −0.16 (0.10; −0.31 to +0.01) after deducting SBC from free cash
flow. Under E2 it is −0.29 and −0.22, and under the imputation bounds −0.39 and −0.36 on the GAAP
basis and −0.25 and −0.26 after SBC. Across χ from 0.80 to 0.95 and both windows the E1 slope lies
between −0.45 and −0.20 on the GAAP basis and between −0.32 and −0.09 after SBC, and across all four
views and both bases between −0.52 and −0.09; the order-m slopes are −0.22 and −0.15.
     Two claims follow, with different strength. That the slope is far above −1, the one-for-one trade that the
Rule-of-40 line describes, holds in all four views and survives Holm’s correction at a familywise level of
5% on both bases. That it is negative holds in sign in all four views, and its 90% interval excludes zero in all
four views on the GAAP basis and three after SBC; but it does not survive the familywise correction (Holm-
adjusted p-values 0.26 to 0.45 on the GAAP basis and 0.35 to 0.62 after SBC). Among the best-performing
software firms, then, a point of growth has come with well under a point less margin, about a fifth of a point
in the baseline. The local conditional quantile, reported as a descriptive statistic, is steeper: −0.41 (0.14) on
the GAAP basis and −0.35 (0.11) after SBC under E1, and −0.61 and −0.43 under E2. It is steeper because
it compares margins at a given growth rate, where the typical fast grower is less profitable than the typical
slow one, while the frontier compares the best of each.
     Did the trade-off steepen? Within periods the cells at 60% growth are too thin, so we compare periods
on the 0–40% window. On the GAAP basis the Ψ0.90 slope was −0.22 (0.10) in 2010–19 and −0.60 (0.11)
in 2020–25, a difference of −0.38 (0.13; −0.60 to −0.16). The difference is negative in all four views, and
its 90% interval excludes zero in three of them (under E2 it runs from −0.66 to +0.02). Its Holm-adjusted
p-value is 0.037 to 0.060 (Romano–Wolf 0.064), so it survives at a familywise level of 10% under every
procedure and at 5% under two of the four. After SBC there was no steepening: the difference is −0.19
(0.14) under E1 and positive in the other three views. The GAAP frontier steepened after 2020; the cash
frontier did not.
     The cross-sectional slope is far flatter than the slope of any firm’s own frontier at its chosen point, which
by Lemma 2 equals −X, and X ≥ 1 for a firm that values a point of growth at least as much as a point
of margin. Proposition 3 explains why the two can differ: every firm faces a downward-sloping frontier,
but the estimated frontier is drawn through the points firms chose, not through their possibilities, and when
firms with more growth opportunity also have higher margin intercepts the chosen points slope down much
less than any individual frontier. The cross-section does not identify any firm’s marginal cost of growth
(Proposition 3(iv)); a common frontier with high opportunity can produce a similar slope. Within firms,
margin and growth are nearly unrelated: with firm and year fixed effects the coefficient of GAAP margin on
growth is +0.06 (0.06), and after SBC −0.03 (0.04). In the model, spend this year buys growth next year, so
we also relate this year’s margin to next year’s growth: the coefficients are −0.03 (0.05) and −0.06 (0.04)
under E1, and −0.15 (0.07) and −0.14 (0.06) under E2 when both years belong to the organic sample. These
are small. Proposition 3(v) shows that anticipated changes in opportunity would produce a strongly negative


                                                       39
within-firm slope, so the weak relation suggests that growth surprises against costs already committed, or
demand shocks that also raise margins, offset much of the cost of buying growth; the regressions do not
identify which.

5.4   What the data do and do not say about the growth technology
The model’s growth technology has two parameters, demand pull and the spend elasticity α, and the data
do not identify them separately. Under Lemma 2, an optimizing firm spends σ ∗ = α(X − k)∆∗ , so the ratio
of spend to excess growth reveals at most the product α(X − k), and only if spend and excess growth are
measured as the model defines them. With selling and marketing expense as the measure of spend, the
median ratio of the previous year’s selling-and-marketing share to growth in excess of the year’s median,
among firm-years growing faster than the median, is 3.02 (0.19) under E1, 3.47 under E2 and 3.34 and 3.15
under the imputation bounds. This spend ratio is robust across the four views, at 3.0 to 3.5, and we report it
as a statistic of its own.
     It does not pin down α(X − k), because the answer depends on how excess growth and the spend that
buys it are measured. By terciles of excess growth the ratio is 10.1, 3.0 and 1.5; among firm-years growing
more than 10 or 20 points above the median it is 1.9 and 1.3; netting out the median selling-and-marketing
share of firm-years growing at or below the median, as maintenance spend, gives 0.99; regressions of the
selling-and-marketing share on growth, which use the variation in levels, give 0.05 to 0.24; and replacing
the year’s median growth by a fixed market growth of 5, 8 or 12% gives 2.1, 2.4 and 2.7. Across methods
α(X − k) ranges from 0.05 to 10.1 under E1, so for α between 0.25 and 0.45 the implied X − k ranges
from about 0.1 to about 40. The data therefore do not identify the weight on growth that firms act on, and
we withdraw the first version’s statement that it lies between 7 and 13. What remains is the theoretical
distinction. The weight a value-maximizing firm puts on growth is the marginal value of a dollar of revenue
(Proposition 2), a valuation multiple, while an investor’s screen applies a duration-based weight of two to
three; the two need not coincide, and the cross-section of chosen points cannot reveal either. We treat α as
a calibration parameter in [0.25, 0.45] and anchor the calibration of Section 7 on observed firms rather than
on a regression.

5.5   Scale
Lemma 3 predicts that the maximal score falls as market share rises. The evidence is weak and not robust.
After SBC, the 95th percentile of the score is 60.1 for firms with $0.1–0.5 billion of prior revenue, 55.3 and
54.5 in the next two classes and 47.4 above $10 billion. A 0.95-quantile regression on log prior revenue
gives −4.1 points per tenfold increase (standard error 3.4) under E1, −1.1 (3.9) under E2 and −2.5 and
−2.8 under the imputation bounds; with period effects the E1 and E2 gradients are −1.8 and +0.6. The
90% interval includes zero in every view, with and without period effects, and on the GAAP basis the E1
gradient is +2.4. Size is in any case a noisy proxy for market share, and larger firms enjoy operating leverage
that the model omits, so the data do not test saturation.

5.6   Capital intensity: an accounting effect
Lemma 4 implies that on a cash basis capital intensity lowers the maximal score in proportion to growth,
while operating margins are affected only through depreciation. We split each period at its median ratio
of capital expenditure and capitalized software to revenue, re-estimating the split in every bootstrap draw,

and compare the 95th percentile of the score. In 2023–25 the difference between the capital-intensive half
and the rest is −12.5 points after SBC (38 against 51; −20.1 to −3.9), an interval that excludes zero in all
four views, but only −1.8 points on the GAAP basis (−9.5 to +4.5). The gap is mostly mechanical. The
sorting variable is deducted from the cash margin, and adding it back leaves −4.6 points (−14.6 to +6.0)
under E1 and −0.2 under E2; sorting on each firm’s median intensity, which a single year’s spending cannot
move, gives −4.5 (−13.6 to +4.9). The 2016–19 gap behaves the same way (−15.6 points, and −10.8
with the deduction added back). The prediction that the gap grows with growth is not supported either: in
a 0.90-quantile regression of the cash margin, the interaction of growth with high intensity is −0.14 (0.19),
negative in only two of the four views. We therefore report the cash-basis gap as an accounting effect of
deducting capital spending, not as evidence for Lemma 4. CoreWeave, described in Section 4, illustrates
how large that deduction can be.

5.7   The distance to the frontier
The stochastic frontier measures how far the typical firm sits below the frontier. We estimate one frontier
per period, common to all cohorts, and the conditional mean of each firm-year’s shortfall given its residual
(Jondrow et al., 1982). The mean shortfall on the GAAP basis was 25.6 margin points in 2016–19 and
28.6 in 2023–25. In 2023–25, the shortfall of firms that entered the sample in 2019 or later exceeds that of
earlier cohorts by +13.8 points on the GAAP basis (+8.8 to +19.4) and +10.2 after SBC (+6.4 to +14.5),
with an interval excluding zero in all four views. Firms present in both periods did not fall further behind:
their shortfall changed by −2.4 points (−7.5 to +3.5). Two caveats apply. Only 72 of the 129 firms that
entered before 2019 and appear in 2016–19 remain in 2023–25, and their 2016–19 median GAAP margin
was 3.2%, against 0.7% for those that left, so the comparison sets survivors against entrants whose exits are
not yet observed. And the shortfall is a distance below a parametric frontier at given growth, not a measure
of managerial inefficiency.
     Because operating margin is gross margin minus selling and marketing, research and development,
general and administrative and other operating expense, the margin gap between firms near the frontier
and typical firms at the same growth rate splits exactly into gaps in each line. Within each growth band
we compare firm-years in the top decile of GAAP operating margin with those between the 40th and 60th
percentiles, for the firm-years with growth of 0 to 60% that report all four lines. On average across bands the
top decile earns 34.5 points more margin [+28.6, +37.7] under E1 (32.7 under E2). Selling and marketing
accounts for 16.0 points [+12.4, +20.0], research and development for 7.1 and general and administrative
expense for 5.5; the gross-margin part, 5.7 [−1.4, +9.4], is not robust, with an interval that includes zero
in every view. The composition changes with growth. Among firms growing less than 10% a year, gross
margin accounts for 14.4 of 36.4 points. Among firms growing 40 to 60%, selling and marketing accounts
for 29.4 of 41.3 points [+18.8, +36.2] and research and development for another 11.9: the best fast growers
buy the same growth with far less selling and marketing expense, which in the model is higher demand
pull. The average gap and the selling-and-marketing part among fast growers survive Holm’s correction
at a familywise level of 5%. The decomposition is descriptive. Firms near the frontier may spend less on
selling because their products pull demand, not because they sell more efficiently, so the decomposition
shows where margins differ, not which changes would close the gap. It agrees, however, with the ordering
of Corollary 3: the growth engine matters most for fast growers and overhead for slow growers, while the
corollary values savings in cost of revenue only to the extent pricing lets the firm keep them.

5.8    The calibration anchor
Section 7 anchors the calibration on the firm-years at or above the 95th percentile of the cash score in 2023–

it computes the growth surplus Π, the excess of the score over its saturated level, under two bounds on how

+26.5] and +18.5 under E2 [+6.8, +26.0], with an interval above zero in all four views. When all of it

across bounds and views, so the data do not determine it.

5.10    Summary
Six facts carry into the rest of the paper; each holds under observed scores, the organic sample and both
imputation bounds unless stated otherwise. (i) Among the best-performing software firms a point of growth
has come with well under a point less margin: the slope of the 0.90-quantile frontier is −0.22 on the GAAP
basis and −0.16 after SBC in the baseline, far above −1 in every view; its negative sign holds in every view
but does not survive the familywise correction. Because the cross-section records choices, the slope does
not measure any firm’s own trade-off. The GAAP slope steepened after 2020; the cash-basis slope did not.
(ii) The data identify neither the curvature of the growth technology nor the weight on growth that firms
act on: the spend ratio of 3.0 to 3.5 is robust, but the product α(X − k) it is meant to reveal ranges from
0.05 to 10.1 across methods. (iii) A decline of the upper tail with scale is weak and not robust. (iv) The


                                                       42
lower cash-basis scores of capital-intensive firms are an accounting effect of deducting capital spending. (v)
Between 2016–19 and 2023–25 the 95th percentile of the score fell by 11.9 points on the GAAP basis and
12.7 after SBC, as fast growers became scarcer, with no detectable change in the best margins at a given
growth rate; it recovered in 2025. (vi) Firms near the frontier earn about 34.5 points more operating margin
than typical firms at the same growth rate, mostly through lower selling, research and overhead costs, and
recent entrants sit further below the frontier than earlier cohorts. Whether the best scores of 2023–25 are
above their durable level depends on how much selling spend buys growth; the data say only that they are if
little of it does.


6     Measuring the Score When Growth Is Bought
The frontier of Lemma 1 prices growth bought with expensed spend at diminishing returns within one
market, and the score budget of Proposition 1 limits how long a firm can score above its saturated level. A
firm that grows by acquiring other firms buys growth with capital instead, across many markets. This section
shows how the Rule of X should be measured for such a firm and brackets the score of a serial acquirer that
listed in 2026. Section 4 flags acquisition-affected firm-years and Table S2 repeats the main results without
them; Proposition 8 says why such firm-years are not points on the frontier, and Remark 4 how to score
them when they are kept. A companion paper (Bangalore Sreenivasa Murthy, 2026) treats the acquirer’s
transformation economics; nothing below depends on it. Proofs are in Section S4 of the Supplementary
Appendix.

Intuition. Growth bought with capital is the model’s growth capital (Assumption 4) at the price paid for
revenue rather than at k. It enters the score at the net weight X − kacq , exactly as the net value of a unit
of organic growth is X − k (equation (3)). A Rule-of-40 screen that adds acquired growth to an operating
margin therefore credits every acquisition, whatever it cost; for X = 1 any purchase above one times revenue
lowers the score that charges its price in full. Because acquisitions in other markets do not draw down the
headroom of the markets the firm is already in, the transience result of Corollary 1 binds each business,
not the firm, and a high score sustained by acquisition is evidence of access to targets and capital, not of
a frontier above the ceiling of Corollary 4. Part (f) is why that access matters: a firm whose base shrinks
and that wins no new customers must keep buying to stand still. Acquirer returns are persistent across deals
(Golubov et al., 2015), but bidders that buy often gain on private and subsidiary targets and lose on public
ones (Fuller et al., 2002), and a high and uneven acquisition rate lowers performance (Laamanen and Keil,
2008), so the supply ḡacq at a given kacq need not last.

7     How High Can the Score Go? Limits by Horizon
We now combine the model with the estimates to bound the score under full automation and to trace a central
path to 2028, 2031 and 2036. Throughout we use the free-cash-flow-after-SBC basis and X = 1, the Rule
of 40 itself. The data do not identify the spend elasticity (Section 5), so the central path uses α = 0.35 and
the sensitivities its range [0.25, 0.45]. Whether the limit is a Rule of 100 or a Rule of 1000 has a precise
answer in the model: it depends on the margin intercept, market growth, capital intensity, demand pull and
the spend elasticity, and automation moves only some of them. How fast a firm approaches the limit depends
on its capacity to execute risky transitions (Section 3.10). Every number in this section is conditional on the
scenario values it states. The replication package grounds each parameter in an assumptions register with
four classes (sourced, grounded by precedent, calibrated from our own data, scenario values), and Table S7
of the Supplementary Appendix lists the source behind each.

7.1   How AI enters the parameters
Remark 1 maps the effects of AI onto the model’s parameters; the companion paper (Bangalore Sreeni-
vasa Murthy, 2026) derives the mapping from the tasks a firm automates. Automation of general, adminis-
trative and maintenance work lowers the non-discretionary cost a and raises the margin intercept m0 = γ − a,
but it adds the recurring costs of running AI (Definition 3). Inference costs enter gross margin γ through the
compute share cc . A survey of about 300 executives at software companies building AI products puts the
gross margin on those products near 45% in 2025 and projects 53% in 2026 and 59% in 2027 (ICONIQ,
2026). That is the cost structure of AI products sold to customers, including talent and hosting, not the
cost of automating a firm’s own work; it is below the median gross margin of 74% in our core sample in
2023–25, consistent with automation shifting cost from operating expense into cost of revenue rather than
removing it. The price of reaching a fixed level of benchmark performance has fallen by between 9 and
900 times a year depending on the threshold (Cottier et al., 2025), but competition may pass much of that
decline to customers, so its effect on γ depends on pricing power (Lemma S2). Whether the compute share
declines depends on how fast the volume of inference per task grows against the fall in its price, for which
there is no market-level estimate: the preliminary elasticity of about −1.1 across providers of the same open
model (Demirer et al., 2025) measures substitution between providers, and its authors report that aggregate
elasticities are substantially smaller. AI can raise demand pull λ h for firms whose products substitute for
labor and market growth G where spending on wages converts into spending on software, and firms that own
computing capacity raise k. At given prices, automation raises the saturated score by 1 − τ points for every
point of cost it removes net of the costs it adds, up to the ceiling of Corollary 4; under free entry with cheap
replication and close substitutes it does not, because the margin it creates is competed away (Proposition 4).

7.2   The ceiling under full automation
Corollary 4 bounds the saturated score of a firm at the full-automation cost structure: one that has automated
everything it can, spends nothing on growth and holds a saturated share. It is a bound at that cost structure,
not on observed scores: a firm with leaner costs than the scenario, in particular a lower compute share,

                                                      49
can score above it. Its parameters are brackets, not estimates. Table 4 evaluates it at 2025 prices, and the
compute share by horizon follows below.
    Accountability floor. The floor amin collects the costs of being accountable that automation does not
remove. They are largely fixed in dollars, so as a share of revenue they shrink with scale (Remark S1(a)).
We use a band from 0.5% to 4.6% of revenue and the values 1.5%, 3% and 4.6% in the favorable, central
and conservative cases. The upper end was set at the fifth-percentile share of general and administrative
expense of firms with $1 to 10 billion of revenue in the wider universe of software and internet registrants;
in our prepackaged-software sample that percentile is 5.8%, so even the conservative case assumes that a
firm of that size spends less on general and administrative expense than 95% of the sample does today. The
central value is close to the tenth-percentile share of firms with more than $10 billion of revenue in our
sample (2.9%), so it assumes that none of that expense is automated. Proposition 5 grounds the lower end
from below. Internal control over financial reporting is a pass-fail standard, so the floor is the cost of the
cheapest control design that leaves no reasonable possibility of an undetected material misstatement, not a
trade of control cost against expected loss. In the example of Remark 6, a firm with $1 billion of revenue has
a bottom-up floor of about 0.23% of revenue: direct audit and Section 404 costs of about 0.14% plus about
0.090% for the cheapest admissible design at the central threshold, under which a design is admissible if it
leaves at most a 5% chance a year of an undetected material misstatement: one human layer over one AI
layer (one over two, about 0.095%, at the stricter 1%). No design that uses AI layers on a single provider
alone passes, however many layers it stacks, because their common-mode failure is more than remote; that
requirement, unlike a headcount, neither cheaper inference nor scale removes. Each further AI application
in the scope of internal control adds a line of IT general controls of about 0.020% of revenue at $1 billion,
or about 0.04% if it also needs a manual fallback for the close, so the floor reaches the band’s lower end
at about 15 such applications per $1 billion of revenue, or 8 with fallbacks (Bangalore Sreenivasa Murthy,
2026). The lower end is thus conditional on how many in-scope AI applications a firm runs.
    Taxes, fees and compute. The cash tax rate τ of 15 to 21% runs from the 15% corporate alternative
minimum tax to the 21% federal rate of 26 U.S.C. 11(b), and excludes state taxes; average effective rates
of profitable large corporations were lower still, 9 to 16% in 2014–18 (U.S. Government Accountability
Office, 2022). Fees f of 2 to 3% of revenue are those of card processing. The compute share cc has no
measured analog, so it is a scenario parameter. At 2025 prices we use 6% of revenue in the central case and
3% and 12% in the favorable and conservative cases: labor costs of 60% of revenue replaced by compute
at a tenth of their cost in the central case, which is above both the cost of today’s agent runs and the field
evidence of about three cents of AI spending per dollar of displaced freelance spending (Stevens, 2026). The
credible bounds run from 1%, labor replaced at the cost of today’s agent runs, to 30%, the value test. On
METR’s software tasks more than 80% of successful runs cost less than 10% of the human cost at $143.61
an hour (Kwa et al., 2025), and that runs cost about 1% of it is our computation from METR’s public run
data (METR, n.d.). The value test caps the share because a task is automated only while its compute costs
less than the labor it replaces. A survey puts the cost of revenue of AI products sold to customers at 55%
in 2025 (ICONIQ, 2026), but that includes talent and hosting and so overstates the compute cost of a firm’s
own automation; the same survey puts spending on AI for internal productivity, direct and indirect and not
itemized, at 11% of revenue in 2025. We keep a high-compute case of 40%, roughly the cost of revenue
implied by the gross margins projected for AI products in 2027. It exceeds the value-test cap of 30%, so it
describes a firm whose revenue comes from AI products rather than a feasible full-automation cost structure.
For comparison, the median core software firm’s entire cost of revenue was 26% of revenue in 2023–25.

    By horizon and with pass-through. Along the scenario path of the compute share, the share moves with
two prices. The price of a fixed level of performance falls by 5 to 10 times a year, while the cost of running
frontier models on the hardest tasks has risen by 3 to 18 times a year, through token volume rather than
price per token (Gundlach et al., 2025). The path prices the work that only frontier models reach near each
horizon at a third of the labor cost it replaces, within the value test, and the rest at the falling price of fixed
performance; because the frontier-priced share of automated work falls as the feasible set grows, the central
compute share rises to 12% in 2028 and then falls to 8% in 2031 and 5% in 2036, with the favorable and
conservative cases at half and twice these values. The central threshold is then 36.0% in 2028, 32.6% in 2031
and 30.1% in 2036, against 31.0% at 2025 prices. These thresholds hold output prices fixed. If competition
passes part of the fall in unit costs to customers, they rise. We let a share ϕ of the variable-cost saving pass
to prices across the industry, of which a firm keeps 1 − ϕ(1 − H) at a concentration H of 0.25; savings in
non-discretionary cost are kept. With ϕ = 0.5 the firm keeps 0.63 of the saving and the central threshold at
2025 prices rises to 36.3%; with ϕ = 0.7 it keeps 0.48 and the threshold is 38.5%. By horizon, with ϕ = 0.5,
the thresholds are 40, 37 and 36%. The scenario values of ϕ span the evidence: close to zero in the short
run for differentiated software (Choi, 2025), 0.7 for an industry-wide cost shock (Ganapati et al., 2020), and
near one between providers of the same model (Demirer et al., 2025).
    Three conclusions follow. First, at the full-automation cost structure, complete automation does not
carry a mature firm past a cash-basis Rule of 100 unless its capital needs are negligible and its market grows
faster than about 31% a year, if compute absorbs 6% of revenue at 2025 prices; a firm whose costs are
leaner than that structure can score more. Every threshold is conditional on the scenario compute share, the
range over its credible bounds is wide, and pass-through raises every threshold. Second, a Rule of 1000
is not a steady state. It requires roughly 9 to 20 times the demand pull of the best public software firms
today, and Proposition 1 and Corollary 1 bound how long such a surplus can last: every point of score above
the saturated level is paid for out of a finite budget of share headroom. Section S5 of the Supplementary
Appendix works through the numbers. Third, under free entry the margin intercept is not set by automation.
With free entry and replication cost approaching zero, Proposition 4 gives R = G + (r − G)(k + ka ) + ρ for
the Rule of 40, where ka is customer capital per dollar of revenue. With market growth of 5%, a cost of
capital of 10%, k = 0.5 and no customer capital, a firm without a scarce input scores 7.5; customer capital
adds a normal return on what it cost to acquire, not a rent. A rent cannot lift the score above the full-cost
ceiling (Proposition 4(d)): at the central cost structure of Table 4, a firm with 5% market growth and k = 0.5
scores at most the central margin term plus 2.5 to 3 points, so a cash-basis score of 100 is out of reach
whatever the rent. In a competitive market, a durable high score requires a rent on an input rivals cannot
replicate, up to that ceiling, and the score does not distinguish such a rent from market power.

figures would suggest for two reasons: they are on an annual cash basis after SBC, and the fastest-growing
AI firms of 2025 and 2026 are private and therefore outside the anchor.

7.4   Levers
The model turns the question “what does it take to approach the limit?” into conditions on parameters, each
of which can be checked against a firm’s own data. Six levers move the limit:
   1. A scarce, non-replicable input that supports a rent ρ: proprietary data with a feedback loop, distribu-
      tion, a license, or network effects. Under free entry, without it the score falls toward G + (r − G)(k +
      ka ). A data advantage is durable only if the data are renewed or the market tips: accumulating data
      has increasing returns early but diminishing returns in the long run (Farboodi and Veldkamp, 2021),
      while data-driven markets can tip to a single firm (Prüfer and Schottmüller, 2021).
   2. Demand pull λ h well above that of the typical firm, which shows up as growth that does not require
      rising selling-and-marketing intensity.
   3. Capital that earns its keep: either k near zero, or capital whose operating return exceeds the condition
      in Corollary 2, so that replacing labor with computing capacity raises rather than lowers the cash-basis
      score.
   4. Pricing that captures value as inference costs fall and as customers’ headcount, and with it their seat
      counts, shrinks, so that falling unit costs raise γ rather than being passed through to customers.
   5. A small accountability floor amin and small full-cost floors. Certification of financial statements, audit,
      model-risk validation and liability insurance remain human-accountable costs; Proposition 5 shows
      that part of the floor is a requirement on the diversity of the control design and on its IT general
      controls rather than a headcount. The costs of maintaining, securing and supervising AI behave in the
      same way.
   6. High market growth G, meaning a position where spending on labor is converting into spending on
      software.
A further factor moves the speed of approach rather than the limit: the capacity to execute. Change capacity,
decision bandwidth and security review bound how many transitions a firm can run, and failures, rollbacks,
leadership changes and reorganizations waste that capacity (Proposition 6 and Proposition 7). For a firm
that acts on a decision weight that falls with share (Proposition 2, under its hypothesis (H)), Corollary 3
orders the levers it controls by stage: while it has share to gain, the productivity of its growth engine
(lever 2) and its retention, the firm-level counterpart of market growth (lever 6), move its score most; once
it is near saturation, overhead and the accountability floor do (lever 5); and capital-heavy automation is
cheapest for slow growers (lever 3). No lever is sufficient on its own. Because any surplus above the durable
score is transient, a sustained cash-basis Rule of 100 requires the levers jointly to push the durable score
R∞ = m0 + (1 − k)G above 100.

    The empirical evidence suggests which levers bind today. Among the best performers a point of growth
has come with well under a point less margin, and the fall in the top of the score distribution between
2016–19 and 2023–25 reflects a change in composition, as fast growers became scarcer, while the best
margins attainable at a given growth rate showed no detectable change. This points to the supply of growth
opportunity (levers 2 and 6), rather than willingness to trade margin for growth, as the constraint that binds,
although the intervals are wide. The decline of the upper tail with size is weak and not robust, so the data
say little about saturation (lever 2, through h), and the lower cash-basis scores of capital-intensive firms are
an accounting effect of deducting capital spending, not evidence on lever 3.

8     Discussion
8.1   Two weights on growth
The model separates two weights on growth that practice treats as one. The screening weight of Lemma 5
compares a persistent change in growth with a persistent change in margin; it is a duration, about 1.8 to
2.8 for a firm with a ten-year runway and partly transitory margins, close to the weights of two to three
that Deeter and Bondy (2024) recommend for free-cash-flow margins “if growth is efficient”. Under the
verification hypothesis (H) of Proposition 2, the decision weight is the marginal value of a dollar of revenue,
the marginal q of revenue in the sense of Hayashi (1982); the worked example gives 5 to 15. It exceeds the
screening weight when the firm’s revenue multiple does, which is a statement about valuation, not about the
frontier. The data do not settle it: the ratio of growth spend to excess growth bounds the net weight from
below for a given measure of spend, but the measure moves the ratio from 0.05 to 10.1 (Section 5). Because
cash flows of longer duration earn lower expected returns (Weber, 2018; Gormsen and Lazarus, 2023), a
constant discount rate, if anything, understates the decision weight. The screening weight tells an investor
how to compare firms; the decision weight tells a manager whether a dollar of growth spend creates value.
    The difference has a practical consequence. A firm that acts on its decision weight X spends σ ∗ = α(X −
k)∆∗ (Lemma 2), so its Rule-of-40 score exceeds the saturated level m0 + (1 − k)G by ∆∗ (1 − k − α(X − k)),
which is negative whenever α(X − k) > 1 − k. If its spend ratio equals the median of 3.02 and it is optimal,
an asset-light firm growing 10 points faster than its market scores about 20 points below the score it would
earn with no growth spend, even though the spending creates value at its own weight. Among the best
firm-years of 2023–25, a quarter score at least 12 points below their durable level if all of their selling and
marketing expense buys growth (Section 7.3). A fixed threshold on the Rule of 40, whether 40, 50 or 60,
therefore marks down firms whose growth spending earns more than it costs, and is better used to screen
firms than to set their spending. Remark 3 gives the same point in value terms: the Rule of X at the firm’s
own weight is a criterion of economic profit plus growth at its net value, while the cash Rule of 40 weights
growth at 1 − k whatever it earns.




                                                       59
8.2   Execution sets the pace
The static frontier says where a firm can be once a transformation is complete; the attainable frontier says
how fast it gets there. In our calibration the binding constraint is the capacity to change the work of employ-
ees, not decision bandwidth or security review, and failures, rollbacks, leadership changes and reorganiza-
tions act like a cut in that capacity (Proposition 7). The difference between central and fast execution, about
41% of the program’s benefits realized by 2036 against the whole program embedded by 2032, lies in the
success rate of transitions and the stability of their sponsorship, not in technology. Two implications follow.
A firm in the middle of a program scores below its eventual durable score by the dip of the transitions in
flight, about 2.5 to 5.1 points in our scenarios, so a screen that reads the dip as weakness penalizes firms that
are executing. And ownership changes the pace more than the level: a public firm that holds to a guided dip
of one point of revenue realizes 32% of the benefits of the central program by 2036 instead of 41%. These
statements are conditional on scenario values for program size, embedding times and the rates at which new
leaders keep programs, which no source we found measures.

8.3   Measurement at the limit
Near the top of the distribution, accounting choices move the score by more than operating performance
does. Four mechanisms matter. First, adjusted margins exclude SBC: Palantir’s second-quarter 2026 score
was 155 on its adjusted operating margin of 62% but 140 on its GAAP operating margin of 47% (Palantir
Technologies Inc., 2026). Its annual GAAP score for fiscal 2025 was 88, but that comparison also reflects an
acceleration of growth, not measurement alone. Second, computing capacity that a firm owns is capitalized.
It enters operating income only through depreciation over the useful life management assigns, it never enters
EBITDA, and it appears in full only in free cash flow. Lengthening the assigned useful life of servers raises
operating margins without changing cash flow, and capitalizing internally developed software shifts cost
between periods in the same way; for a firm whose capitalized spending grows with revenue, amortization
charges today’s income with past, smaller vintages, so the GAAP score exceeds the cash score permanently,
by more the faster the firm grows (Bangalore Sreenivasa Murthy, 2026). Third, recording revenue gross
rather than net raises reported revenue relative to profit, and it raises reported growth while the mix of gross-
recorded revenue is rising. Fourth, run-rate and annualized quarterly revenue run ahead of annual revenue
in a growth spurt. Each of these can push a reported score above the full-automation ceiling without any
change in economics. The ceiling itself is a bound at a stated cost structure, not on observed scores: a firm
whose costs are leaner than that structure, for example with a lower compute share, can score above it in
economic terms too (Corollary 4).
     Growth that is bought also needs care: acquired revenue enters a reported score without the capital paid
for it, and interest on acquisition debt is not in the operating margin, although under U.S. GAAP it is in
operating cash flow; Section 6 shows how to charge both without charging financing twice. For Bending
Spoons, on figures from its F-1 filed on 8 June 2026, which are unchanged in the final prospectus, the 2025
GAAP score of 115.9 falls to 105.0 after interest, and to between 87 and 114 when acquisitions are charged
as capital against a margin before interest, depending on the charging convention. Such a score is evidence
of access to targets and capital, not of a frontier above the ceiling, and a firm whose base shrinks and
that wins no new customers must keep buying to stand still. This is why statements about reported scores
use observed scores, while a statement about the frontier is made only where it also holds on the organic
frontier, which removes acquired growth. We suggest computing the score as this paper does, on annual free
cash flow after SBC and after capital expenditure including capitalized software, and, where lease-financed


                                                       60
capacity is material, also after additions to finance-lease assets.

8.4   What to track if headcount falls
The Rule of 40 was built for firms whose main costs were people, so its implicit trade-off is between selling
and marketing expense and growth. When automation turns labor into capital, capital becomes the scarce
input, and the score has no capital term. Two firms can both score 100 while earning very different returns on
the capital that produced the score. Remark 3 says what to track instead: economic profit, defined as after-
tax operating profit minus the cost of capital on invested capital including leases and purchase commitments,
plus growth at the net weight X − k. Two further supplements follow from the model: gross profit per dollar
of computing cost, because the compute share is the largest uncertainty in the ceiling; and the growth-
weighted score with a weight that reflects the firm’s own marginal value of revenue (Proposition 2) rather
than a fixed screening weight.

8.5   The accountability floor
Full automation does not remove the cost of being accountable. Under Section 404 of the Sarbanes–Oxley
Act, management must assess the company’s internal control over financial reporting every year, and the
auditor of an accelerated filer must attest to that assessment in an integrated audit performed under PCAOB
Auditing Standard 2201 (United States Congress, 2026b; Public Company Accounting Oversight Board,
2026). Emerging growth companies are exempt from the auditor’s attestation, and a newly public com-
pany’s first annual report need not contain management’s assessment (United States Congress, 2026b; U.S.
Securities and Exchange Commission, 2026a), so the floor of a young public firm is lower than that of a
seasoned one. Sections 302 and 906 make the principal executive and financial officers personally respon-
sible for certifying the periodic reports (United States Congress, 2026a). Regulators, insurers and courts
look to identifiable people and entities. The recurring costs of this system (control testing, the integrated
audit, directors’ and officers’ insurance and the legal and compliance staff behind them) form the floor amin
in Corollary 4. Much of the floor is fixed in dollars, so as a share of revenue it falls with scale: in the core
sample the median general and administrative expense, which contains most of it, is 20% of revenue for
firms with less than $1 billion of revenue in 2023–25, 12% between $1 and $10 billion and 7% above $10
billion, and Microsoft and Oracle report 2.4 to 3.6%. Section 7.2 sets the band.
     Automated controls can lower the cost of testing, but the floor need not fall with automation and may
rise with it. Internal control is a pass-fail standard, so Proposition 5 gives the floor as the cost of the cheapest
admissible control design, one that leaves no reasonable possibility of an undetected material misstatement,
rather than as a trade of control cost against expected loss. When control layers share one AI model, they
share its failures, so the common-mode part of the miss probability does not vanish however many layers
are added, and it does not fall with scale. In the illustrative example of Remark 6, no design built only from
AI layers on one provider is admissible at either threshold, and the cheapest admissible design is one human
layer over one AI layer at the central threshold and over two at the stricter one. Each AI application that
enters the scope of internal control also adds its own IT general controls. Diversity of the control design
and the quality of IT general controls, not headcount, therefore decide admissibility, and firms that can bear
accountability credibly and cheaply hold a scarce input in the sense of Proposition 4.




                                                        61
8.6   Limitations
The evidence covers firms that file XBRL financial statements with the SEC under US GAAP, mostly U.S.
firms. The fastest-growing AI firms of 2025 and 2026 are private and absent, so the sample likely understates
the current frontier at high growth; this is one reason the 2028 to 2036 path comes from the calibration rather
than from the sample. Industry membership uses each registrant’s current SIC code; the rule on a firm’s
principal business could be checked only for the firms the review panels named, and its keyword classifier
misses several of them, while the rule on listed equity flags firm-years rather than excluding them. Both
enter only as sensitivities. The cross-sectional frontier records the points firms chose, so it does not identify
any firm’s own trade-off (Proposition 3); the data do not identify the spend elasticity α or the weight on
growth that firms act on; and the decision weight’s interpretation rests on hypothesis (H), which we state
but do not prove. The ceiling rests on a compute share with no measured analog and on scenario values
for loss severities and the build stock; the pace rests on scenario values for program size, embedding times
and the persistence of sponsorship; and the central thresholds hold output prices fixed, with pass-through
as a scenario. The central path and its sensitivities are conditional on the stated parameter assumptions and
should be read as scenarios, not forecasts.


9     Conclusion
The Rule of 40 is a line, not a frontier. Read through a model of the attainable set, it is a price at which growth
is traded for margin, and the maximal score is reached where a line at that price touches the frontier. Each
firm’s frontier is concave, with an intercept set by the margin it earns at market growth and a slope set by the
diminishing returns to buying growth. In value terms the Rule of X is a rule of economic profit plus growth
at its net value, which carries the frontier beyond software under the conditions of Section 3.11. The frontier
that can be estimated across firms is drawn through the points firms chose. In SEC-registered software firms
from 2010 to 2025, among the best performers, a point of growth has come with about a fifth of a point less
margin, far from the one-for-one trade of the Rule-of-40 line, in observed scores, on the organic frontier and
under both imputation bounds. Between 2016–19 and 2023–25 the top of the distribution of scores fell by
11.9 to 12.7 points as fast growers became scarcer, with no detectable change in the margins attainable at
a given growth rate, and it recovered in 2025. The data do not identify the weight on growth that firms act
on, the curvature of the growth technology or a decline of the score with scale, and the lower cash scores of
capital-intensive firms are an accounting effect.
     The answer to “Rule of 100 or Rule of 1000?” is neither, as a steady state. Automation raises the margin
intercept by removing operating cost, but it adds computing cost and the recurring costs of maintaining,
securing and supervising AI, and, for firms that own capacity, capital that weighs on cash-basis scores. Sat-
uration removes the growth surplus as markets fill, and within one market a finite budget of share headroom
limits how long any surplus lasts. Under free entry, competition removes the margin that automation creates
unless the firm holds a scarce input, and even a rent cannot lift the score above the full-cost ceiling. A
sustained cash-basis score above 100 therefore requires near-complete automation, negligible capital needs
and a market growing at a rate few markets sustain: about 31% a year if compute absorbs 6% of revenue
at 2025 prices, between 27 and 51% over the credible range of that share, and more if cost savings pass to
customers. Scores of several hundred are possible only briefly, in new markets with exceptional demand
pull. Capacity to execute risky transitions limits how fast a firm gets to its ceiling: under our scenario as-
sumptions an exceptional firm realizes about 41% of the benefits of its automation program by 2036 under


                                                        62
central execution, having embedded about 61% of it, and embeds all of it by 2032 under fast execution.
On the central scenario path its maximal score is about 56 in 2028, 60 in 2031 and 66 in 2036. These are
scenarios, not forecasts.
     For practitioners the implications are concrete. Compute the score on annual free cash flow after stock-
based compensation and capital expenditure, and charge acquired growth with what it cost, charging either
interest or a cost of capital on the capital that paid for it, not both. Use the Rule of 40 to screen firms, not
to set growth spending: a firm that values a dollar of revenue at its marginal value should accept a lower
Rule-of-40 score while its growth spending earns more than it costs. Read cross-sectional comparisons as a
record of choices by firms with different opportunities, not as the trade-off any one firm faces. Supplement
the score with economic profit (Remark 3) and gross profit per dollar of computing cost as headcount stops
being the main cost. And evaluate AI strategies by the parameters they move: the margin intercept, demand
pull, capital intensity, pricing power, the accountability floor, the full-cost floors of maintaining, securing
and supervising AI, and the capacity to execute the transitions that get the firm there. For a firm whose
decision weight falls with share, the first target of a fast grower is the productivity of its growth engine and
its retention, and that of a mature firm is overhead; savings in cost of revenue are worth only what pricing
power lets the firm keep; owned capacity pays only where growth is slow enough for the cost it removes to
exceed growth times the capital it adds; and control designs that rely on one model leave a requirement that
more layers do not meet. Under free entry, the limit of the rule is set by what stays scarce, not by what is
automated.

Remark 6 (The accountability floor in numbers). The example follows the companion paper (Bangalore Sreeni-
vasa Murthy, 2026); its values are illustrative. A firm with $1 billion of revenue has one in-scope control objective
with No = 24 opportunities a year for a material misstatement. Human control layers fail with probability 0.05 and
cost $500,000 a year each, with a common-mode probability of 0.002; AI layers on one provider fail with probability
0.02 and cost $50,000, with a common-mode probability of 0.005 given its IT general controls; those controls and the
AI application’s revalidation cost $350,000 a year; and there is no cross-type term and no separate cost of errors below
materiality. The standard sets no number for “remote”. We take π̄ = 5% a year as the central threshold and π̄ = 1%
as a stricter sensitivity. The central value rests on how filers fare under the standard. Adverse auditor attestations were
4.6% of all SOX 404(b) attestations for fiscal 2020 (Hallas, 2021) and 5.8% for fiscal 2021, against 4.8% for 2019, in
the latest edition we could read at the source (Audit Analytics, 2022). Most audited filers, whose controls are mostly
run by people, therefore obtain effective opinions, and a threshold at which the all-human reference design of this
example fails would contradict that evidence; 1% does so here. By Proposition 5, no all-AI design on the provider
is admissible at either threshold: however many layers it uses, it lets at least one material misstatement through in a
year with probability at least 1 − 0.99524 ≈ 11.3%. All-human designs pass at the central threshold with three layers
(5.0% a year, $1.50 million a year); every all-human design leaves at least 1 − 0.99824 ≈ 4.7%, so none passes at
1%. At the central threshold the cheapest admissible design is one human layer over one AI layer (3.1% a year, $0.90
million, about 0.090% of revenue); at the 1% threshold it is one human layer over two AI layers (0.7% a year, $0.95
million, about 0.095%). With 48 opportunities the central design needs a second AI layer ($0.95 million); with an AI
common-mode probability of 0.01 it still passes (3.6% a year). At the 1% threshold either change requires two human
layers over one AI layer ($1.40 million). That no all-human design passes at 1% signals that a human common-mode
probability of 0.002 per opportunity is high for a firm whose controls are effective, or that 1% is stricter than the
standard as it is applied: the parameters illustrate the mechanism and are not calibrated to an effective control environ-
ment. The expected-loss form of the floor in an earlier version of this paper charged $10 million for each undetected
misstatement and compared designs, two of which leave a reasonable possibility of a material misstatement; under the
standard that loss does not enter the floor, and only the requirement does. Direct audit and Section 404 costs add about
0.14% at this size, for a floor of about 0.23% at either threshold. An IT-general-controls line like that of an application
performing invoice matching and exceptions costs about 0.020% of revenue a year. It covers access over the appli-
cation’s identities and tool permissions, change management, operations, four interfaces into in-scope systems, and a
retest of its controls at each model upgrade, every 1.22 years. The retest costs $24,000: 10 controls at the 16 hours per
control test reported in a survey of SOX programs (KPMG, 2025), at $150 an hour. It covers control testing only, not
the functional revalidation of the build counted in cmnt . A manual fallback for the close costs about $161,000 more, so
the floor reaches 0.5% at about 15 in-scope AI applications, or 8 with fallbacks. code/verify_theory.py checks
these values.

B     Human–AI Research Protocol
This appendix documents how the paper was produced. arXiv’s policy is that generative AI tools cannot be authors,
that authors must report significant use of such tools, and that authors take full responsibility for all content however it
was generated (arXiv, 2023, 2026). We go further than a disclosure statement because the collaboration was structured,
and its structure is part of what a reader needs to judge the work. All work took place on 28 and 29 October 2026;
times are those of the session clock (UTC−7), checked against the platform’s UTC timestamps. Section S7 of the
Supplementary Appendix gives the detailed records: every agent with its model and contribution, the sequence of the
work, the review panels and the errors caught by each safeguard.

B.1    Agents and models
The human author posed the research questions, set the scope, made every decision listed in Table 9, reviewed the
manuscript and is responsible for it. All AI work was done by agents built on Anthropic’s Claude models inside
one research session. The session was configured for the model identifier claude-opus-5-5, which agents inherited
unless another model was chosen for them; after the author asked for different models to be used (13:08 on 29 October),
the cross-model referees and the second assumptions auditor ran on the model fable and the literature agent of the
second version on sonnet. A lead agent coordinated the work and was the only agent that communicated with the
author. Where independence mattered, an agent was given a distinct role and no access to the others’ outputs. The
agents fell into five groups. Drafting and integration: the lead agent, which planned the work, retrieved the data,
built the panel, ran the first estimation and calibration and drafted the first version, and an editor-author agent, which
assembled and revised the paper and generated every quoted number from the analysis outputs. Theory: theory
agents, an integration theorist and an accounting and internal-control agent, which wrote the model and its proofs and
the script that verifies them (verify_theory.py). Data, estimation and parameters: an estimand econometrician
and then a sample-audit agent, which built the audited sample and its inference; data and reliability-evidence agents,
which sourced the full-cost parameters; two assumptions auditors, which graded every parameter by its grounding;
a parameters agent, which adopted the re-grounded values in one commit; and an acquirer case agent. Literature:
three literature agents, which searched for and verified every reference. Review: a council of three agents on the first
answer, a referee panel of three and two audit rounds on the first draft, and twelve panels on the first version. The
project later grew into three papers: this one, a companion paper on the economics of AI transformation (Bangalore
Sreenivasa Murthy, 2026), and a paper on the software engine.

B.2    Review rounds
A council of three agents critiqued the lead agent’s first answer to the research question without seeing each other’s
critiques; four of their changes survive here (capital intensity in the frontier, very high scores at large scale during
market transitions, the measurement basis and accountability costs that scale with the damage a failure can cause).
A referee panel of a theorist, a financial economist and an econometrician, and an audit agent, then reviewed the
complete first draft; their reports led to the rebuilt panel as first reported, sample-size-invariant statistics, the treatment
of α as a calibration range, the dynamic weight of Proposition 2 and the firm-by-firm inversion of Section 7. The
first complete version was then reviewed on a frozen snapshot, taken at 12:46 on 29 October, by twelve panels: four
academic referees and four practitioner panels on claude-opus-5-5, and four cross-model referees in the same fields
on fable, told not to read the other reports, with a second assumptions auditor on fable. Each practitioner panel
was a single agent playing several named roles, so the panels are structured self-critique, not a set of independent
human experts. All twelve reports recommended major revision; no referee found a false theorem in the static model,
while the theory referees found an upper bound of Proposition 7 that fails when reorganizations arrive at random and a

a revision plan, on which the author took four decisions (D-1 to D-4, Table 9). In the second version a sample-audit

B.5    Safeguards and errors caught
Six safeguards address the known failure modes of AI-generated research. Fabricated citations were addressed by
dedicated verification passes with a recorded outcome for every reference. Errors in derivations were addressed by
a script that checks each closed form, first-order condition and envelope derivative symbolically and checks shape
properties numerically on random parameter draws (verify_theory.py), and by referees who re-derived the results
independently. Errors in data handling were addressed by spot checks of the panel against filed figures for named firms,
by reporting several margin definitions, samples and estimands, and by a replication package. Errors in quoted numbers
were addressed by generating every number in the abstract, introduction, data, empirical and horizons sections and
conclusion from the analysis outputs (paper1_macros.py). Confirmation bias was addressed by independent critics
who did not see one another’s work, by an assumptions register that classifies every parameter by its grounding, and by
reporting results that contradicted earlier drafts. The risk that reviewers share the drafters’ blind spots was addressed
by running referees on a second model. Table S9 in the Supplementary Appendix lists the errors that affected this
paper’s content, grouped by the safeguard that caught them.

B.6    What the protocol does not establish
Independent agents built on the same underlying model may share blind spots, so their agreement is weaker evidence
than agreement among human experts would be. Running referees on a second model reduces this risk but does not
remove it, because all the models come from one developer. The econometrics referees on the two models, working
without sight of each other’s reports, reached the same main findings by different routes. Several parameters remain
scenario values. The author’s review is the final check, and responsibility for any remaining error is the author’s.
