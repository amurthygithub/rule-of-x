## 1. Summary and recommendation

The revision addresses most of what the two round-1 reports in this field asked for. The Demirer et al. elasticity is now read as a preliminary within-model, cross-provider substitution elasticity in both places where it was misused; the compute share is re-grounded at 6% of revenue at 2025 prices with a stated path by horizon and with METR, ICONIQ (with scope), both halves of Gundlach et al. and Stevens cited; a pass-through dimension has been added; the free-entry benchmark now states its assumptions, carries customer capital and a full-cost bound, and withdraws the infeasible 92.5-point rent example; the horizon upper ends of 156 and 123 are gone and a central path with a one-at-a-time tornado replaces them; the AI-failure floor uses no-review error rates with a common-mode term; the Kim et al. figure is used only as P(same wrong answer | both wrong); and every external parameter is sourced in Table S7 with what it measures and a grounding class. The headline numbers now reproduce exactly from the replication code, and the "scenario, not forecast" conditionality is carried through the abstract, introduction and conclusion for the ceiling and the path.

Three new problems remain, all in the bolt-on parts of Section 7.2 rather than in the model, and all fixable without new data:

1. **The pass-through sensitivity is implemented incorrectly.** The firm's retained share of a cost saving is applied as a loss of margin share at the old revenue, which is what the paper's own Lemma S2 warns against: a pass-through *rate* (Ganapati et al.'s 70%, the DMA study) is plugged into a formula that needs the pass-through *elasticity*, and the price cut's effect on the revenue denominator is ignored. On the paper's own scenario values the central threshold with pass-through is about 32%, not 36.3% (ϕ = 0.5) or 38.5% (ϕ = 0.7); the intro's "36% if half of the industry's cost savings pass to customers" is wrong in both number and description. The retention rule 1 − ϕ(1 − H) is also undefined and uncited in Paper 1, and the exclusion of non-discretionary savings contradicts Proposition 4(d).
2. **Demirer et al. is misused again**, now in Section 7.2 and Table S7 as evidence of pass-through "near one between providers of the same model". The paper does not analyze pass-through, and it documents substantial, persistent price dispersion across providers of technically identical open models, which cuts the other way.
3. **The upper end of the "credible range" of the compute share (30%) does not follow from the test the paper states.** "A task is automated only while its compute costs less than the labor it replaces" caps the share at the automated labor share, 60% of revenue, not 30%; the 30% comes from an undocumented factor of one half (`CC_VALUE_TEST_CAP = s_L × 0.5`), which the assumptions memo attributes to a "review share" that a no-review ceiling does not have. The abstract's "27 to 51%" would read "27 to 77%" under the stated test, and the labelling of the 40% high-compute case as infeasible depends on the same factor.

Beyond these, the moderate findings concern conditionality: the horizon path of the compute share is driven entirely by two unstated class-d scenario values while the sourced price trends are immaterial to it; the 2025-price headline (31%) is not a date on the paper's own path, which does not reach a 6% compute share until about 2034; the "largest swing comes from market growth" statement is true by construction on an unmotivated band; one quoted sensitivity still uses human catch rates that the ceiling no longer uses; the two labor bases of the ceiling (60% for compute, 30% for error losses) disagree; and a stale sentence states transition costs of two years of savings where the code uses 1.25.

**Recommendation: minor revision.** The theory in this field (Proposition 4 with customer capital and the full-cost bound, Proposition 5, Lemma 6, the attainable and expected frontiers) is sound and the calibration is transparent and reproducible. Findings 1 to 3 change numbers that appear in the abstract and introduction and must be corrected and re-run; the remaining items are wording, conditionality and a few sensitivities. I would want to see the corrected pass-through numbers and the resolved value-test cap before acceptance, but I do not think a further full round is needed.

Severity counts for new findings: **3 major, 8 moderate, 9 minor** (20 findings).

## 3. New findings

### N1. [Major] The pass-through sensitivity applies a pass-through rate where the paper's own Lemma S2 requires an elasticity, and ignores the revenue denominator; the thresholds with pass-through are overstated by about 4 to 6 points

- **Location.** §7.2 "By horizon and with pass-through" (p. 52); intro ("if half of the industry's cost savings pass to customers it rises to 36% at 2025 prices"); §7.3 "With pass-through of ϕ = 0.5 the central path is 56, 59 and 64 ..."; Table 6 row "Pass-through ϕ = 0.7"; `calibrate_horizons.py` `base_phi()`: `c['base'] - (1 - c['tau']) * (1 - dur(phi)) * (U0 - c['c_c'])`.
- **Issue.** The code subtracts the passed-through dollar saving, measured as a share of today's revenue, from the margin share. A price cut lowers the revenue denominator by the same dollars, so the margin *share* falls by much less. Lemma S2 says exactly this: dγ/du = −(1 − ϕ)/p holds for ϕ the pass-through *elasticity* d ln p/d ln u, and "the pass-through elasticity is not the pass-through rate: dp/du = ϕ p/u". The evidence the paper cites for its ϕ values (Ganapati et al.'s 70% of a cost change passed to prices; the DMA study's share of products with price cuts) are pass-through rates. With the non-fee unit cost at 23.5% of price (U0), a rate of 0.525 (= 0.7 × (1 − 0.25)) is an elasticity of 0.12, not 0.525. The paper's formula therefore inflates the effect by roughly 1/(u/p) ≈ 4.
- **Evidence (reviewer's arithmetic, the paper's own inputs).** At the central cost structure the pre-tax margin term is 0.842. Passing 0.525 of the 17.5-point variable saving to prices cuts the price by 9.2% of today's level; holding compute and the floors fixed in dollars per unit and fees proportional to price, the margin share becomes (0.842 − 0.092)/0.908 ≈ 0.826 (0.828 with the fee adjustment), a fall of 1.4 to 1.6 points pre-tax, not 9.2. After tax the central threshold is about 32.1% at ϕ = 0.7 and 31.7% at ϕ = 0.5, against the paper's 38.5% and 36.3%. The same correction shrinks the by-horizon thresholds (40/37/36 at ϕ = 0.5) and the central-path effect (66 → 64/63) to about one point.
- **Two further problems in the same paragraph.** (i) The retention rule "a firm keeps 1 − ϕ(1 − H) at a concentration H of 0.25" is neither derived nor cited in Paper 1; the code comment traces it to the companion paper and the engine. H = 0.25 is a class-d engine default. (ii) "Savings in non-discretionary cost are kept" is an assumption that contradicts Proposition 4(d), which puts a_min and c_full inside the unit cost that competition prices, and §7.1's statement that under free entry "the margin it creates is competed away". It also means the sensitivity covers only the 17.5-point variable saving and not the 15.5 points of non-discretionary saving (reviewer's arithmetic from the anchor's implied a of 22.8% to a_min + c_full of 7.3%) that the program mostly consists of. Under the corrected arithmetic, exposing all savings at a rate of 0.525 gives a threshold of about 33%, so the practical consequence is small once (i) is fixed, but the assumption must be stated.
- **Required change.** Recompute the pass-through cases with the margin share at the post-cut revenue (or, equivalently, convert the cited rates into Lemma S2 elasticities, ϕ_elas = rate × u/p) and correct the numbers in §7.2, §7.3, Table 6, the introduction and `macros.tex`. Use a different symbol for the rate and the elasticity. State the retention rule as a scenario, derive it in a line or cite its source. Either apply the pass-through to all cost savings or state why non-discretionary savings are not exposed, with the alternative reported. Replace the intro's "half of the industry's cost savings" with what the scenario does.

### N2. [Major] Demirer et al. is cited for near-complete pass-through between providers of the same model; the source does not analyze pass-through and documents the opposite pattern

- **Location.** §7.2: "The scenario values of ϕ span the evidence: ... and near one between providers of the same model (Demirer et al., 2025)"; Table S7, row "Identical models, near 1, Demirer et al."; `notes/lit_pass_v2.md` A1 ("which the source supports").
- **Issue.** A within-model own-price elasticity of −1.1 says that a provider's token volume falls about proportionally with its own price. It is a demand-side substitution estimate and says nothing about how providers pass cost changes into prices. Moreover the paper reports "substantial variation in prices across providers for open-source models", coefficients of variation "reaching as high as 1.7", calls this "striking, given that the underlying models are technically identical across providers", and finds price related to throughput. Persistent dispersion for identical products is evidence of provider differentiation and pricing power, which argues against near-one pass-through, not for it. The paper itself contains no pass-through analysis (author PDF, checked 29 Oct 2026; Section 4).
- **Required change.** Delete the clause and the Table S7 row, or replace the anchor for "near one" with the VAT standard-rate evidence already in Table S7 (Benedek et al.) or with the homogeneous-Bertrand limit stated as theory. Correct the lit-pass note. This is the second round in which this source is characterized for something it does not show; the next version should quote the exact sentence relied on for every use of it.

### N3. [Major] The 30% upper bound of the compute share does not follow from the value test as stated; the abstract's "27 to 51%" and the infeasibility label of the 40% case rest on an undocumented factor of one half

- **Location.** §7.2 "Taxes, fees and compute": "The credible bounds run from 1% ... to 30%, the value test. ... The value test caps the share because a task is automated only while its compute costs less than the labor it replaces"; "It exceeds the value-test cap of 30%, so it describes a firm whose revenue comes from AI products rather than a feasible full-automation cost structure"; Table 4 notes; abstract and conclusion ("27 to 51% over the credible range"); `calibrate_horizons.py`: `CC_VALUE_TEST_CAP = CC_PATH['s_L'] * 0.5`; `notes/assumptions_round2.md` §2 ("kappa_front = 0.33 [0.10, 0.50], class d bounded by the value test kappa < 1 − review share").
- **Issue.** With automated labor at s_L = 60% of revenue, "compute costs less than the labor it replaces" is κ < 1 and caps c_c at 60%, not 30%. The 30% is s_L × 0.5, and the memo's rationale for the 0.5, "1 − review share", is a property of the companion paper's task model in which human review remains; the ceiling of this paper has no human review (Lemma 6(c), Remark 5). The factor appears nowhere in the paper.
- **Evidence (reviewer's arithmetic).** At c_c = 0.60 the central threshold is 76.6% (c_anc rises with c_c), so the stated test gives a credible range of 27 to 77%, not 27 to 51%. At the stated test the 40% high-compute case (threshold 60%) is feasible and should not be labelled infeasible.
- **Required change.** State the cap and its derivation in the text. If the intended test is that compute must cost less than half the labor replaced (for example because transition costs, maintenance and error losses must also be covered, or because the firm requires a margin of safety), say so and ground the half; otherwise set the upper bound at 60% and restate the range in the abstract, §7.2 and the conclusion, and relabel the 40% case.

### N4. [Moderate] The horizon path of the compute share is set by two class-d scenario values that the text does not state, and the two measured price trends it cites are immaterial to it

- **Location.** §7.2 "By horizon and with pass-through" (first five sentences); Table S7 rows "Fall in compute price at fixed performance" (class a) and "Rise in cost of frontier runs" (class d); `calibrate_horizons.py` `CC_PATH`.
- **Issue.** The path is c_c(H) = s_L[ω_H κ_front + (1 − ω_H) κ_fixed(H)] with κ_front = 0.33 and ω_H = 0.6, 0.4, 0.25 (the share of automated labor cost in tasks "reached within two years of H"), both class d. At the sourced decline of 5× a year, κ_fixed is 0.08% of labor cost by 2028 and at its floor thereafter, so for any D in the sourced range [3, 10] the path is c_c(H) ≈ 0.2 ω_H: the 12/8/5% sequence is the ω sequence times a constant. The Gundlach 3–18× rise is cited as if it drove the path ("the share moves with two prices") but is not an input; the cap replaces it. The text gives "a third" for κ_front but never states ω_H, and Table S7 lists the two price trends as the path's sources.
- **Required change.** State ω_H and κ_front in §7.2 with their class, say that the path is a mix scenario and that the fixed-performance price decline does not affect it beyond 2028, and report the threshold's sensitivity to ω and κ_front (for example ω_2028 ∈ {0.3, 0.9} gives c_c of 6% and 18%, thresholds of about 31% and 41%). Drop the implication that the measured trends determine the numbers.

### N5. [Moderate] The 2025-price headline is not a date on the paper's own path, and "no measured analog" is retained beside a measured analog

- **Location.** Abstract and conclusion ("about 31% a year if compute absorbs 6% of revenue at 2025 prices"); §7.2; §8.6 ("The ceiling rests on a compute share with no measured analog").
- **Issue.** The path's own logic is that in 2028 60% of the automatable work is frontier-priced at a third of its labor cost, so the compute share at full automation is 12% in 2028, 8% in 2031 and 5% in 2036; it passes through 6% only around 2034. "Full automation at 2025 prices with a 6% share" is therefore a hypothetical cost structure that combines today's price per unit of fixed performance with a capability set that the path says does not exist until the mid-2030s. The conditionality ("if compute absorbs 6%") is stated, but the abstract leads with the number the paper's own path does not support for a decade, while the near-term threshold on that path (36%) appears only in the intro and §7.2. Separately, §7.2 says the compute share "has no measured analog, so it is a scenario parameter" two sentences before quoting ICONIQ's 11% of revenue on internal AI; round 1 asked for the phrase to go.
- **Required change.** Lead with the path (36, 33 and 30% in 2028, 2031 and 2036) in the abstract, or say that 6% at 2025 prices is a hypothetical cost structure reached on the path only in the 2030s. Delete "no measured analog" in §7.2 and §8.6 and say instead that the analogs (METR run costs, Stevens, ICONIQ internal spend, the inference share of AI-native products) bracket the value.

### N6. [Moderate] The ceiling uses two different bases for the labor it automates

- **Location.** §7.2 ("labor costs of 60% of revenue replaced by compute"); Table 8 ("W_A = 30%" for errors, misbehavior and common-mode losses); `calibrate_horizons.py` `CC_PATH['s_L'] = 0.60`, `FC['A_auto'] = 0.30`.
- **Issue.** Compute is charged on 60% of revenue of automated labor; error, misbehavior and common-mode losses are charged on 30%. Both describe "the automated work" of the same full-automation firm.
- **Evidence (reviewer's arithmetic).** At W_A = 0.60 the central c_err rises from 1.93 to 3.86 points, c_full from 4.3 to 6.2, and the central threshold from 31.0% to 32.5%.
- **Required change.** Use one base, or state which part of the 60% carries consequential errors and why.

### N7. [Moderate] The "highest measured error rates" sensitivity still uses human catch rates that the ceiling no longer uses

## 5. What should stay

The free-entry result is now a clean special case of Mankiw–Whinston with customer capital and the full-cost bound (Proposition 4(d)), and the paragraph that distinguishes a normal return on customer capital from a rent is exactly right. The accountability floor as a pass/fail control standard with a cross-type common mode (Proposition 5) is the paper's most portable contribution in this field and is stated carefully. The no-review treatment of c_err, the explicit scope of ICONIQ and METR, and Table S7 with grounding classes and "what the source measures" columns are the kind of disclosure an AI-economics audience will want to see more of. The reproducibility is exemplary: every headline number in this report's field reproduces from `calibrate_horizons.py` to the decimal.
