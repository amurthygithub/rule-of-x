Lemma S1 (Timing of growth capital).     (a) Equation (2) is exact in continuous time. In discrete time it holds ex-
    actly when capital is installed one period ahead, It = k(St+1 − St ), with g the forward growth rate.
  (b) If capital is installed contemporaneously, It = k(St − St−1 ) with g = St /St−1 − 1 > −1, then m = mop − kg/(1 +
      g), and the frontier becomes m̃(g) = m0 − kg/(1 + g) − κ(g − G)β . The two conventions differ by kg2 /(1 + g) ≥
      0, which is second order in g; at k = 0.5 and g = 0.3 the gap is about 0.035.
  (c) If α < 1/2 and k > 0, then m̃ is strictly convex on an interval (G, G + ε), and the feasible set under convention
      (b) is not convex, so the Rule-of-X problem can have several local maxima. For example, take α = 0.3, k = 1.25,
      κ = 0.5, G = 0.1, m0 = 0.5 and X = 1. Then the corner g = G is a local maximum of Xg + m̃(g) (score 0.486),
      and there is a second, interior local maximum at g ≈ 0.74 (score ≈ 0.595). Under (2) the same parameters
      give the unique optimum g = G, because X < k.
  (d) The condition in (c) is sufficient, not necessary. If α = 1/2, then m̃ is strictly convex exactly on (G, (k/κ)1/3 −1),
      which is nonempty if and only if k > κ(1 + G)3 . If α > 1/2, m̃ is strictly concave near G, but it is strictly convex
      on an interval whenever 2k(g − G)2−β /(1 + g)3 > β (β − 1)κ at some g > G, which holds when k is large
      relative to κ. For an asset-light firm the non-convex region is a sliver: with α = 0.3, k = 0.05, ℓ = 0.86 and
      G = 0.1 it ends about 2 points of excess growth above G.

    We adopt (2) because it is exact in continuous time, matches capacity that must be in place before revenue arrives,
and keeps the feasible set convex for every α ∈ (0, 1) and every k ≥ 0.
Remark S2 (Binding financing cap). If σ ∗ > σ̄ , the optimum is at the cap: σ = σ̄ , g = G + ℓσ̄ α and R∗X = m0 + (X −
k)G + (X − k)ℓσ̄ α − σ̄ , because σ 7→ (X − k)ℓσ α − σ is strictly concave and increasing below σ ∗ . Where the cap
binds strictly, the envelope formulas of Lemma 4 continue to hold with (σ ∗ , ∆∗ ) replaced by (σ̄ , ℓσ̄ α ).
Remark S3 (The Rule of 40 is a pass mark, not a choice rule). Along the frontier, g 7→ g + m∗ (g) is strictly concave.
The frontier points that pass the Rule of 40 therefore form an interval, possibly empty, and if the interval is nonempty it
contains the Rule-of-1 optimum. The rule accepts a range of strategies, and a point that passes may still be dominated.
The Rule of X, by contrast, selects a single point: the tangency.

Remark S6 (Mode of competition). The conclusion L → 0 in Proposition 4 requires post-entry competition that does
not punish a second entrant too severely. Under homogeneous Bertrand competition with any sunk cost F > 0, a second
entrant would earn zero and lose F. A single firm therefore enters (provided monopoly profit covers the entry cost)
and charges the monopoly price for every F > 0, and incumbency itself becomes the scarce input. Under contestability
(a fixed flow cost F̂ that is not sunk), price equals average cost, so the markup is F̂/(Px) and vanishes as F → 0, as
under Cournot. A firm that rents all of its capital and pays for its customers as a flow has k = ka = 0 in (24), so its
competitive-limit score is XG. With differentiated products under monopolistic competition (Dixit and Stiglitz, 1977),
free entry dissipates profit through the number of varieties, while the markup over marginal cost stays at the level that
substitutability between varieties sets; the limit L → 0 then fails, and the competitive-limit score includes that markup.

S2     Further Discussion of the Model
This section collects discussion that accompanies the results of Section 3 of the main paper.

S2.1    Conventions for growth rates
This subsection accompanies the setup of Section 3.1. The static results can equally be read in period rates, provided
the growth technology (1) is specified in the convention the score uses; it cannot hold in both at once. If it holds in
continuously compounded rates, the frontier with k = 0 expressed in annual rates turns convex once excess log growth
exceeds β − 1 (between 1.2 and 3 for the range of α used in the paper), so a maximal score in annual rates exists only

S2.4     The assumptions behind free entry
This subsection accompanies Proposition 4. The result is specific to its assumptions, three of which do most of the
work. Entry happens only at date 0: with G > 0 flow profits grow without bound, so later entry would be profitable for
any F, and allowing it would drive the markup to zero faster, not slower; the conclusion is conservative in that direction
only. The scarce input’s supply grows at exactly the market rate, a knife-edge case that keeps its price constant; with
slower supply growth the rent would rise over time. And the product is homogeneous: with differentiated products
the markup stays at the level that substitutability sets (Remark S6). Nor does the model let firms respond to cheaper
replication by spending more on quality or growth, which can keep concentration high when such sunk outlays are
chosen, as in the theory of endogenous sunk costs (Sutton, 1991). Rents can also accrue upstream: the supply of
foundation models has strong economies of scale and scope (Korinek and Vipra, 2025), so a scarce input may belong
to the model provider and appear as a markup inside cc (υc in Remark S11) rather than as the software firm’s ρ.

S2.6    Common-cause failure across providers
This subsection accompanies Assumption 7 and Proposition 5. The assumption treats failures of different types as
independent apart from the cross-type term pcc . That is a simplification, and it favors diversity. Errors of models
from different providers are correlated: across 71 models on 14,042 four-option MMLU questions from HELM, when
two models are both wrong they give the same wrong answer about 60% of the time on average, against one third
for uniformly random wrong answers (Kim et al., 2025). That figure is the probability of the same wrong answer
given that both models are wrong; it is not the probability that one model errs given that the other does, which it
does not measure. For a control that flags an item when two providers disagree, it means that about 60% of joint
errors pass unflagged. We therefore read the value of diversity in Proposition 5(a) as an upper bound, and we treat
a second provider as a separate type only to the extent that its failures are not common with the first. The common-
mode probabilities are also conditional on the IT general controls: a firm that weakened those controls would raise
them. The cross-type term covers management override, defective shared data, failures of the IT general controls and
reviewers’ acceptance of wrong AI output, not shared tooling as such: an identity provider, an endpoint security agent
or a cloud region that every layer depends on can defeat layers from different providers at once. The model has no
term for that dependence, so the diversity rule addresses the failures of models, not the availability and tooling risk of
the infrastructure beneath them, and the full-cost floor does not include the cost of keeping a second provider ready.

S2.7    The program clock
This subsection accompanies Definition 4. Three features of part (b) are assumptions, and each has a stated reason.
The uniform clock is a mean-field reading of reorganizations: Proposition 7(f) shows that it is the almost-sure long-run
limit of a clock with random freezes, and states the results that hold under random freezes at every date. Disruptions
run in calendar time because the turnover and strategy data that ground νD are rates per calendar year. And a realized
share that a second migration does not change describes the shortfall of a migrated process as a property of the process
(how much of the targeted value the automated work actually delivers), which is how the calibration’s source measures
it: the share of targeted value that transformations had realized once they were done (Bucy et al., 2021). Disruptions
are independent across attempts; if a disruption instead ends every attempt in flight at once, the bounds and equalities of
Proposition 7(c) and (e)(i)–(ii) are unchanged, because they depend only on per-attempt probabilities, but the variance
of the embedded share and of the dip is higher.
     Part (e) is the version that the calibration of Section 7 computes, with cw = ρTpar for a binding capacity ρ expressed

only about 28% of the central program at first, and its budget loosens only as savings accumulate; by 2036 its intercept

number of employees affected. An owner can also raise change capacity, for example by replacing staff, or accept a
larger dip than a public firm guides to; the central case models neither, and this paper does not analyze private-equity
or permanent-capital owners. Under slow execution (5% of employees a year, a share 0.54 of attempts creating value,
37% of the targeted savings realized, frequent disruption), 5.0% of the program’s benefits are realized by 2036.

S2.10     Scores in filings
This subsection accompanies Section 8, “Use in filings”. A score computed on adjusted margins, on free cash flow,
on organic growth or with acquisitions charged as capital is a non-GAAP financial measure when an issuer makes
it public. It then needs the most directly comparable GAAP measure, presented with equal or greater prominence,
and a quantitative reconciliation (U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, 2026b,a; U.S. Securities and Exchange
Commission, Division of Corporation Finance, 2022). A performance measure may not remove items as non-recurring
that are reasonably likely to recur within two years, and a measure that excludes normal, recurring cash operating

expenses can be misleading; for a serial acquirer that question extends to acquisition and reorganization costs. Free
cash flow is a liquidity measure and may not be presented per share (U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission,
Division of Corporation Finance, 2022). Palantir defines its Rule-of-40 score under “Additional Definitions”, outside

S2.11     Ownership regimes
Remark S8 (Ownership regimes). An owner sets the weight X, the discount rate, the horizon and the tolerated dip,
and its decision speed scales the decision capacity; the companion paper (Bangalore Sreenivasa Murthy, 2026) derives
these for public, private, private-equity and other owners. On a common technology, three consequences follow. (i)
Owners with different weights choose different points of the same attainable set: a higher weight picks a point with
weakly more growth, and, when the lower weight is positive and growth is strictly higher, less margin. (ii) An owner
who admits only the transitions that pay within its rate and horizon admits a subset P ′ ⊆ P, whose attainable and
static sets are contained in those of an owner who admits all of P with the same capacities. (iii) Pace differs: a
tighter dip budget, or slower decisions that lower decision capacity, weakly shrink At and weakly lower R̄X (t) at every
horizon (Propositions 6(a) and 7(b)), while the static frontier of a given program is unchanged.

S5     The Rule of 1000
This section works through the second conclusion of Section 7.2 of the main paper. By Lemma 3(d), in continuously
compounded rates, even with a 50-point margin intercept and 20% market growth, a Rule of 1000 requires demand
pull λ h between 6.8 and 9.3 for α between 0.45 and 0.25. The upper-quartile growth surplus of the 2023–25 top tail
(Section 7.3), at the upper bound on growth spend, corresponds to λ h of 0.47 to 0.75 over the same range of α, so a
Rule of 1000 needs roughly 9 to 20 times the demand pull of the best public software firms today. We use only the
upper bound: under the lower bound no selling and marketing expense buys growth, so the surplus is not a return to
spend and implies no demand pull.
     Such pull is necessarily transient. Proposition 1 shows that every point of score above the saturated level is paid
for out of a finite budget of log share headroom, within one market and at given retention, and Corollary 1 turns the
budget into a bound on the number of years a firm can sustain a given score. With a margin intercept of 60 points,
20% market growth and a share cap of 50%, a firm that starts at 1% of its market can score 1,000 in at most 1 year,
and one that starts at a thousandth of a percent in at most 5. A new product in a new market opens a new budget (the
intuition after Proposition 1), and each such episode is bounded in the same way.

S7.2     Sequence of the work
Question and council. The project began with a question the author put to the lead agent: taking the Rule of 40 and
its proposed successors, and assuming everything can be automated with AI, what is the theoretical limit of the score,
how does it evolve over two, five and ten years, and what conditions are needed to approach it. The author asked
for a council of agents. The lead agent gathered current evidence and drafted an answer; the three council members
critiqued it without seeing each other’s critiques. Their critiques changed the answer in four ways that survive in this
paper: growth and margin are linked through capital intensity (Lemma 4); very high scores can occur at large scale
during market transitions, not only at small bases; the measurement basis moves scores by more than operations do
(Section 8); and accountability costs scale with the damage a failure can cause rather than with headcount.
     First draft. The author then asked for the efficient frontier and for a paper suitable for arXiv. The lead agent
checked arXiv’s policies on AI authorship, endorsement and position papers before asking the author four scoping
questions, on the evidence base, authorship, affiliation and the documentation of the AI role. Data retrieval required
the author’s participation: the agents ran in a cloud environment that could not reach the SEC’s servers, so the author
granted the lead agent access to a folder and to the SEC’s websites in a browser on the author’s own computer (Sec-
tion S6). The first draft found a flat cross-sectional frontier with the dominance-cone estimator; the lead agent returned
the result to the theory agent, which added Proposition 3 to show how a flat frontier of chosen points can coexist with
a trade-off for every firm.
     Referee round on the first draft. The referee panel and the audit agent reviewed the complete first draft, and all
three referees recommended major revision. Their reports changed the data (the panel was rebuilt as first reported), the
statistics (sample-size-invariant statistics replaced an edge statistic, and, on the econometrician’s recommendation, a
local conditional quantile replaced the dominance-cone frontier), the identification (α became a calibration range), the
theory (the dynamic weight of Proposition 2 and the firm-by-firm inversion of Section 7) and several claims; Table S12
lists the errors. The steeper slope of the local quantile was then reported as a reversal of the flat frontier. That reading
was wrong: a later review showed that the flat frontier survives the rebuilt data under the original estimator, and that the
change came from the switch of estimator, not from the data. This version therefore reports the theory’s own object,
the quantile frontier Ψχ , as primary, and the local quantile as a descriptive statistic (Section 5). A second audit of the
revised draft returned 33 findings (4 errors, 13 inconsistencies, 5 overclaims, 6 unsupported claims, 5 style issues), of
which 29 were resolved at once and the rest after a re-audit.
     Extension and integration. The author then asked for a model of AI transformation itself, which became the
companion paper, and asked that every constraint found there also act on the frontier. The integration theorist charged
the full costs of running AI against the ceiling, added the attainable and expected frontiers and the generalization to
economic profit, and rebuilt the calibration. The assumptions auditor’s register then required every headline number
that rests on a scenario value to be stated as conditional on it. The author decided to split the project into three
papers and to release this one first; the editor-author agent made it self-contained, replacing every dependence on the
companion paper either by a compact statement with a proof (Lemma S4, Proposition 5, equation (27)) or by a citation
where the argument does not depend on the result.
     Review of the first version and revision. The first complete version was reviewed by twelve panels and a second
assumptions auditor (Section S7.3). The lead agent consolidated their findings into a revision plan, on which the author
took four decisions at 13:53 on 29 October (D-1 to D-4, Table 8 of the main paper). A sample-audit agent rebuilt the
sample under five rules and re-estimated every statistic; a parameters agent adopted the re-grounded parameters in one
commit; a literature agent verified and corrected the citations; theory agents addressed the theory referees’ findings;
and the editor-author agent revised the text on the audited sample, rewrote the horizons section around a central path
and one-at-a-time sensitivities, and split the paper into a main text and this Supplementary Appendix.

S7.4    Errors caught and the safeguards that caught them
Six safeguards address the known failure modes of AI-generated research. Fabricated citations were addressed by
dedicated verification passes with a recorded outcome for every reference. Errors in derivations were addressed by
a script that checks each closed form, first-order condition and envelope derivative symbolically and checks shape
properties numerically on random parameter draws (verify_theory.py), and by referees who re-derived the results
independently. Errors in data handling were addressed by spot checks of the panel against filed figures for named firms,
by reporting several margin definitions, samples and estimands, and by a replication package. Errors in quoted numbers
were addressed by generating every number in the abstract, introduction, data, empirical and horizons sections and
conclusion from the analysis outputs (paper1_macros.py). Confirmation bias was addressed by independent critics
who did not see one another’s work, by an assumptions register that classifies every parameter by its grounding, and by
reporting results that contradicted earlier drafts. The risk that reviewers share the drafters’ blind spots was addressed
by running referees on a second model.

error escapes with probability P(second model wrong | first wrong) × P(same wrong answer | both wrong), and only
the second factor is measured, at about 0.60 on HELM (Kim et al., 2025). The provider-concentration term Hcm of
Lemma S4(e) is a lower bound on the effective concentration, because providers’ errors are correlated and providers
share infrastructure. The companion paper (Bangalore Sreenivasa Murthy, 2026) sources each rate and derives the
floors activity by activity.
Remark S10 (The accountability floor in numbers). The example follows the companion paper (Bangalore Sreeni-
vasa Murthy, 2026); its values are illustrative, and it is not a control-design recommendation: admissibility is decided
by management’s assessment and the auditor’s evidence. A firm with $1 billion of revenue has one in-scope control
objective with No = 24 occasions a year on which a material misstatement arises, and π is the probability that at least
one of them passes every control layer in the year. Human control layers miss with probability 0.05 and cost $500,000
a year each, with a common-mode probability of 0.002; AI layers on one provider miss with probability 0.02 and cost
$50,000, with a common-mode probability of 0.005 given their IT general controls, which cost $204,672 a year, the
line of one in-scope AI application priced at the end of this remark. There is no cross-type term, a human layer misses
the items the AI layers missed at its own rate, and there is no separate cost of errors below materiality.
     The threshold. The standard sets no number for “remote”, so π̄ is a scenario value, and we report π̄ = 5% and
π̄ = 1% a year side by side without choosing either as central. Two facts bear on the choice. First, adverse auditor
attestations were 4.6% of all SOX 404(b) attestations for fiscal 2020 (Hallas, 2021) and 5.8% for fiscal 2021, against
4.8% for 2019, in the latest edition we could read at the source (Audit Analytics, 2022); later editions exist, but
we could not read their figures at the source and do not use them. These rates are an incidence of controls judged
ineffective, not the probability that a misstatement goes undetected, so they bear on the plausibility of a threshold
rather than measure it. In the example, every all-human design leaves at least 1 − 0.99824 ≈ 4.7% a year, so none
passes at 1%, while three human layers pass at 5% by a hair (4.98% a year, $1.50 million). A threshold at which
designs run by people fail would make ineffective control the norm among filers whose controls are mostly run by
people, which the attestation rates contradict; at 1% that is either a sign that the threshold is strict or that the human
common-mode probability of 0.002 is high for an effective control environment. Second, the evidence is at the level
of the firm and the constraint per objective: a firm-level 5% spread evenly over 10 objectives is 0.51% per objective
(0.10% at 1%), at which the cheapest admissible design is 2 human over 1 AI ($1.25 million; 2 human over 2 AI and
$1.30 million at 1%).

Remark S11 (Owning the compute). Let the compute behind cc cost TCO per dollar of revenue a year at cost, of
which servers are the share ssrv , with life Lsrv , and let a vendor charge (1 + υc )TCO. Owning it requires capital
kc = ssrv Lsrv TCO, replaced at the rate 1/Lsrv , and leaves the running cost (1 − ssrv )TCO in cc . Growth capital and
replacement servers are the same equipment and take the same deduction rate ψ (Remark 2). By Corollary 4, owning
changes the ceiling of the Rule of 40 by (1 − τ)υc TCO − (1 − ψ)τssrv TCO − (1 − ψτ)ssrv Lsrv TCO G. Under current
U.S. law domestic servers take the 100% allowance, ψ = 1, and owning raises the cash ceiling if and only if υc >
ssrv Lsrv G, whatever the tax rate. With servers 60% of the cost of compute (Michael and Cottier, 2026) and a 5.5-year
book life, ssrv Lsrv = 3.3, and the markup must exceed 16.5% at 5% market growth and 49.5% at 15%. A firm that
elects out of the allowance, or whose servers are used predominantly abroad, has ψ < 1 and a higher bar; at the lower
bound ψ = 0 and τ = 0.18 the bar is 33% and 74%. Economic obsolescence faster than the book life raises the bar, and
a test of value rather than of the steady-state score charges the capital at r + δK in place of the growth rate (Bangalore
Sreenivasa Murthy, 2026). The comparison assumes that owned capacity is fully used. If it is used a share u < 1 of the
time, its capital cost per unit used is 1/u times that at full use, so the markup at which owning pays rises as utilization
falls; operating staff and power limits, which the remark omits, raise it further.

Accountability floor (amin ). The share of revenue that a fully automated firm still spends on being accountable: offi-

scenario value, and the largest uncertainty in the ceiling.
Decision weight. The weight a value-maximizing firm puts on a point of growth relative to a point of margin: the

it.
Demand pull. How much growth a dollar of growth spend buys at a small market share; better products and channels
have more of it.
Durable (saturated) score. The score a firm keeps once it has no share left to gain: its margin intercept plus market
growth net of the capital that growth needs.
Embedded and realized shares. Of an automation program, the share whose processes have been changed and stay
changed (embedded), and the share of the program’s targeted benefits the firm actually captures (realized), which is
smaller.
Estimands E1 and E2. E1 uses scores as reported; E2 removes growth bought by acquisition. The imputation bounds
add back, with imputed organic growth, the acquisition years E2 cannot adjust.
Familywise correction. An adjustment of p-values for testing many statistics at once, so that the chance of any false
finding in the family stays at the stated level.

Frontier. The best margin a firm can earn at each growth rate. The frontier estimated across firms (Definition 2) is

Ψχ . The paper’s primary frontier statistic: for each growth level, the χ-quantile of the margin among firm-years that
grew at least that fast.
Remote threshold (π̄). In the control model, the largest probability that a design may leave that, over the year’s
opportunities for a material misstatement, at least one passes every control layer. A scenario value, not a tolerance.
Screening weight. The weight on growth that investors use to compare firms, typically two to three; it reflects how
long growth lasts.
Share headroom and the score budget. The room a firm has to gain share in its market. Within one market and at
given retention, every point of score above the durable level uses up part of a finite budget of headroom (Proposition 1).
Spend elasticity (α). How fast the returns to growth spend diminish; the data do not identify it, so the paper uses a
range.
Top-tail firm. A firm at the 95th percentile of the 2023–25 cash score, the starting point of the central path.
T (g0 ). The 95th-percentile margin among firm-years growing at least g0 , plus g0 : a point on the frontier at a fixed
growth rate.
