## (3) Candidate findings

**F1 — "Counting the costs of accountability alone" still counts compute and fees.**
1. Locator: main §7.2, printed p. 56 (= PDF p. 56), sentence "Counting the costs of accountability alone and none of the costs of running AI, the four thresholds would be 21, 27, 36 and 55%"; and Table 4 note (p. 56), "With accountability costs alone, the base would be 79.5, 72.6, 63.5 and 44.7."
2. Description: the thresholds/bases labeled "accountability costs alone" exclude only c_full (maintain/secure/supervise), i.e., they compute 100 − (1−τ)(1 − c_c − f − a_min); compute (6%) and fees (2.5%) remain in the calculation.
3. Classification: minor presentation issue (ambiguity).
4. Evidence: I reproduce all six numbers exactly under 100 − (1−τ)(1 − c_c − f − a_min): central base 0.82×(1−0.06−0.025−0.03) = 0.7257 → 72.6; threshold 27.4 → "27"; favorable 79.5/20.5→"21"; conservative 63.5/36.5→"36"; high-compute 44.7/55.3→"55"; and the sensitivity ends "23 to 72%". Under a literal "accountability alone" reading (a_min only) the numbers would be ~20.5/25.5/42/57.
5. Strongest author defense: the sentence contrasts accountability with "the costs of running AI" — the paper's fixed term for c_full (Definition 3) — and compute is the automation's own input rather than a "cost of running AI" in that sense; the Table 4 note defines "Base" by formula, so the calculation is recoverable.
6. Verdict: survives as wording; the numbers are correct and the intended reading is recoverable, but the phrase invites the wrong reading (a reader may conclude the accountability band alone moves the threshold by ~10 points, when ~9 of those points are compute and fees).
7. Affected conclusions and magnitude: none numerically; presentation only.
8. Smallest correction: "with none of the costs of running AI (c_full), and compute and fees still charged". Acceptance test: a reader with only §7.2 and Table 4 reproduces 21/27/36/55 from the stated parameters.

**F2 — The "no number of AI layers passes" conclusion rests on the beta-factor form's all-layers-defeated common mode, which the cited CCF literature itself flags as conservative at higher redundancy.**
1. Locator: main Prop 5(a)–(b), printed pp. 29–30 (= PDF pp. 29–30); Assumption 7, p. 29; §7.2, p. 55 ("no design built only from AI layers on one provider passes, however many layers it stacks"); supp Remark S10, printed p. 50.
2. Description: in Assumption 7 a type-specific common mode defeats *every* layer of that type at once ("Otherwise all layers of type j fail together with probability p_j^cm"); hence P_o(D) ≥ p^cc + (1−p^cc)p_j^cm for any depth of one type, and an all-AI design on one provider fails whenever p^cm ≥ P̄_o = 0.0021 (5%) — "however many layers."
3. Classification: missing hypothesis (a scope condition the model does not state as a limitation).
4. Evidence: this is the beta-factor form, and the paper says so ("a share of failures strikes every redundant component at once", citing Hokstad & Rausand 2008 and Mosleh et al. 1989/1998). But NUREG/CR-5485 — the paper's own citation — states that "common cause events do not always fail all redundant components" and that beyond redundancy levels of about three or four the beta-factor model "generally yields results that are conservative"; Hokstad & Rausand's chapter is a status review of exactly this limitation and of finer models (multiple beta-factor). If a provider common-mode event instead defeats each layer independently with probability q < 1, the common-mode term of an n-layer design scales like p^cm q^n and enough layers could pass at 5%; the "no all-AI design" corner of Prop 5(b) would then hold only for q near 1.
5. Strongest author defense: the beta-factor form is the standard, most commonly used CCF model (verified), and for *control* layers the "one model provider, one set of ITGCs" architecture makes near-simultaneous defeat of all layers on the provider genuinely plausible (a regression after a model update hits every layer running that model); the paper explicitly scopes the conclusion to the example ("That conclusion is an assumption of the example, and the provider's common-mode probability under the firm's IT general controls is the number to estimate", Remark S10) and gives the realistic lever (ITGC quality θ, not more layers); the direction of the model's bias (conservative for risk) is the safe side for a *floor on control cost*.
6. Verdict: narrowed. Within the stated model the mathematics is correct; what is missing is one sentence acknowledging that the beta-factor form itself is the conservative idealization (per the cited literature) and that the "however many layers" claim is conditional on it.
7. Affected conclusions and magnitude: the illustrative example only (Remark S10 is explicitly not used for the ceiling: the band 0.5–4.6% governs). If q were materially below 1, the cheapest admissible design could in principle become all-AI (≈$50k + ITGC vs $750k), lowering the example floor from ~0.37% toward ~0.30% of revenue; the band, the central 32.5% threshold and all headline results are unaffected.
8. Smallest correction: in Assumption 7's discussion or S2.6, add: "In the beta-factor form a common-mode event defeats every layer of the type; the CCF literature (Mosleh et al. 1998; Hokstad and Rausand 2008) notes this is conservative when redundant components are not always all defeated, so the claim that no number of layers passes is an upper-bound reading, and the provider's common-mode probability given the firm's IT general controls is the number to estimate." Acceptance test: recompute Table S15's cheapest design under a per-layer defeat share q ∈ {0.3, 0.6, 1} and report the range; the printed conclusion must be stated for q = 1.

**F3 — The 1% lower credible bound of the compute share is a per-successful-run cost; no per-completed-process conversion is applied anywhere.**
1. Locator: main §7.2, printed p. 56 ("credible bounds run from 1%, labor replaced at the cost of today's agent runs (Kwa et al., 2025; METR, n.d.)"); supp S2.8, printed p. 8 ("that runs cost 0.4 to 1.1% of it, rounded to 1%, is our computation from METR's public run data"); Table S11, printed p. 37 ("software tasks, 50% success, no review").
2. Description: the 1% anchor measures the compute cost of *successful* runs at METR's ~50% success rates. A firm automating a process also pays for failed attempts, so the compute cost per completed process is higher by roughly 1/success-rate.
3. Classification: evidence-transport problem (a success-conditioned statistic transported as an unconditional cost bound).
4. Evidence: Kwa et al. (verified): "more than 80% of *successful* runs cost less than 10% of what it would cost for a human"; the paper's own 0.4–1.1% is computed from the successful-run subset at 50% success (Table S11 note). **F4 — The payback arithmetic is an expectation-based construction whose two approximations push in opposite directions, and two phrases under-describe it.**
1. Locator: main §7.3, printed p. 61 (= PDF p. 61): "Under central execution realized gains first exceed the dip after about 4.0 years ... the cumulative gains repay the cumulative dip only after 7.5 years, or 8.5 discounted at 9.34% ... 2. (ii) The gain flow uses the (e)(i) upper bound E(t) ≤ min{1, p_emb c_w n(t_R)}, which overstates the greedy plan's embedded share by less than one wave's capacity (1.7% of the program) after waves can no longer be filled — anti-conservative, and disclosed ("overstates by less than b̄ρT_par afterwards", S2.7). ## (5) Open questions for the adversarial auditor

1. **Dispersion of the realized share.** Only the mean b̄ = 0.67 is stated; the distribution of the persistent shares b_p is unspecified, so the 2036 "41%" and the 7.5-year payback are expectations whose sample-path dispersion is unidentified. Under heavy-tailed b_p (most processes realize little), the median firm's realized score would sit well below the mean. Ask for a two-point or Beta sensitivity on b_p.
2. **Stock vs flow reading of change capacity at the fast case.** The model charges capacity at wave *starts*; under the paper's central parameters the in-flight stock is ≈9.2% of the program, below the 10% capacity, so both readings coincide — but the fast case (22%/yr starts, 1.024-year flight) puts in-flight work at ≈22% of the program, exactly at the capacity line. Ask for a stock-constrained variant of the fast case.
3. **The uniform clock at finite dates.** Prop 7(f) proves the uniform clock is the a.s. long-run limit and that the bounds can fail at finite dates; the calibration applies it over an 11-year horizon. How much dispersion does the random-freeze clock add to the 2036 embedded share (the paper's own S2.7 says the expectation is "not the number at the expected program time")?
4. **The beta-factor defeat share.** Quantify F2: at π̄ = 5% and p^cm = 0.005, what per-layer defeat share q would make an all-AI design admissible, and is there any measured analogue (e.g., correlated failure of multiple deployments of the same model during a provider incident)?
5. **Threshold grounding.** Given Appendix A's disclosure that the central threshold was moved after the designs were known, can the Audit Analytics adverse-attestation rates (4.6–5.8%, FY2019–21) be supplemented with a later vintage or a different plausibility anchor (e.g., restatement rates), so the 5%/1% scenario values have at least one input chosen before the example's outcomes?
6. **Interim periods in the admissibility calculus.** The model aggregates over N_o occasions per year for annual reporting; AS 2201's definition covers interim statements, and quarterly-close controls are inside ICFR. Does the per-objective threshold need an interim-quarter reading, or is N_o = 24 already intended to span quarterly closes? (Remark S10 says "24 occasions a year" without saying how they map to the fiscal calendar.)
7. **The 0.4–1.1% METR computation.** The paper's own computation from METR's run data (a class-c input) could be re-run against a newer run-file vintage; does the 1% anchor (and hence F3's correction) move with the 2026 run data?
