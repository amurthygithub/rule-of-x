# Referee report: "The Rule of 40 as a Price Line: Efficient Frontiers, Economic Profit and the Limits of AI Automation" (paper1_v1)

Referee: industrial-organization economist, economics of AI, automation and digital markets. Independent cross-model review; no other review files were read. Materials read: the paper snapshot (paper1_v1.txt and .pdf), sections/model.tex, horizons.tex, discussion.tex, literature.tex, measurement_acquired.tex, appendix_proofs.tex (Lemmas 3 and 4, Remarks 9 to 11), code/calibrate_horizons.py, notes/calibration_results.json, notes/partI_revision_memo.md, notes/assumptions_register.md, notes/assumptions_memo.md, data/tasks/params_reliability.yaml, notes/data_sources_reliability.md, notes/data_sources_part2.md (parameter rows), notes/case_bending_spoons_f1_extract.md, paper1/refs_paper1.bib. Date: 29 October 2026.

## 1. Summary

The paper models the set of attainable (growth, margin) pairs of a firm that buys growth with expensed spend at diminishing returns, reads Rule-of-X lines as price lines, and derives the maximal score as a tangency: a margin intercept, plus market growth, plus a transient growth surplus drawn from a finite budget of share headroom (Propositions 1 to 4). It separates the firm's own growth weight (marginal q of revenue, Proposition 6) from the investor's screening weight (a duration, Lemma 1), shows the cross-section does not identify any firm's frontier (Proposition 7), restates the rule as economic profit (Proposition 13), and estimates partial frontiers on 293 SEC software filers. Two extensions carry the paper's title claim: a full-cost ceiling under complete automation (Definition 3, Lemma 4, Proposition 10, Corollary 4, Table 10) and an attainable frontier whose pace is set by organizational capacity and transition risk (Propositions 11 and 12, Table 11). Section 6 treats acquired growth with a Bending Spoons illustration.

The theory is clean, the measurement discipline (as-first-reported XBRL, two estimands, cash after SBC) is a real contribution, and the paper is unusually explicit that its headline thresholds are conditional on a scenario compute share. My concerns are concentrated in the AI-automation mapping and the horizon calibration, which is the part of the paper an IO reader will take to practitioners. The central problems: (i) the 2028/2031/2036 ranges are envelopes of jointly extreme scenario corners whose drivers (G path, demand-pull multiplier mu, time-invariant full-cost intercept) carry no stated grounding and ignore capability feasibility and inference-price paths entirely; (ii) the two execution-pace parameters that set the pace, the success probability and change capacity, are taken from sources that measure different objects (proof-of-concept attrition and program-level self-reported transformation success; layoff rates) and none of those sources is cited in the paper; (iii) the argument that the compute share need not fall as inference prices fall rests on a market-level elasticity that does not apply to a firm at fixed automation scope; (iv) the error floor uses human review catch rates for a firm defined to have no human reviewers; and (v) the free-entry benchmark has costless customer acquisition, which contradicts the paper's own growth technology and its evidence that firms act on growth weights of 7 to 13. The Bending Spoons arithmetic reproduces from the F-1 extract to the decimal; two framing points need fixing.

## 2. Recommendation

Major revision. The frontier theory, the two-estimand empirics and the acquired-growth measurement are publishable with modest changes. The ceiling and horizon sections need (a) re-sourcing or re-labelling of the pace parameters, (b) a central scenario with one-at-a-time sensitivities (or a Monte Carlo over the register ranges) in place of corner envelopes as the headline, (c) an explicit statement that capability feasibility and inference-price paths are not modelled, and (d) the conceptual corrections in findings 4 to 6 below. None of this requires new data.

## 3. Findings

Severity scale: Major (affects a headline claim or a conclusion), Moderate (affects a stated number or an argument's validity but not a headline), Minor (wording, notation, citation precision).

### F1. [Major] The horizon ranges are scenario envelopes, not bounds, and are grounded in neither METR horizons nor inference-price declines
- Location: Section 7.3, Table 11 (tab:calib), abstract, Section 9; code/calibrate_horizons.py lines 276 to 280 (HZ dictionary) and 296 to 308 (horizon()); assumptions_register.md Section 3 rows "G paths H", "mu pull multiplier H", "m0 paths H".
- Issue: Each range pairs the low (high) end of every scenario dimension at once: anchor quartile, execution case, full-cost intercept, G, mu and alpha. The product of six independent extremes is a hull, not a bound in any probabilistic sense, and it widens mechanically with the number of dimensions. The upper end in 2028 (134 under the upper bound on spend, 156 under the lower) is Pi_2025 upper quartile times 2^(1/(1-0.45)) = 3.5, i.e. a doubling of demand pull in three years with alpha at its top; the paper concedes it "implies growth above 100%". The mu bands (1 to 2, 0.75 to 1.5, 0.5 to 1.25) decline over horizons while the G bands rise to 30% by 2036; nothing in the text says why demand pull should double by 2028 and halve by 2036, or why a saturating firm's market would grow 30% in 2036 when Section 5 reports median growth of 12% and a shrinking supply of fast growers. The register classes all three as "d: scenario" with "no precedent". The full-cost intercept m0F (58, 68) is time-invariant: no inference-price decline (Cottier et al. 2025; Gundlach et al. 2025, both cited elsewhere) enters the 2028 to 2036 path, and nothing from METR task-horizon growth (Kwa et al. 2025, not cited) enters either. The pace depends only on organizational capacity and hazards: the model implicitly assumes all 48 processes are technically automatable from 2026, so "fast execution embeds all of it by 2033" is a capacity statement, not a feasibility statement. The companion paper apparently has a capability path (register rows capability.r_A, capability_path/*); Paper 1 inherits none of it.
- Evidence: calibration_results.json: upper_bound 2028 k=0 Pi = [0, 0.568]; lower_bound 2028 R = [0.399, 1.557]; ceiling['central']['base'] identical in all horizons. Register: "G paths ... d: scenario around the 2025 median of 12.9%"; "mu ... d: scenario".
- Required change: (1) State in Section 7.3 and Table 11 notes that capability feasibility and the inference-price path are not modelled and that the intercept target is held at its 2026 value. (2) Replace the headline with a central path (median anchor, central execution, central ceiling, G at the E2 median, mu = 1) and report one-at-a-time sensitivities to G, mu, alpha, execution case and ceiling; keep the envelope as a secondary table. (3) Justify or drop the mu and G bands; if mu > 1 is meant to encode a transient AI demand boom, say so and bound it with Proposition 4 (see F10). (4) Cite Kwa et al. (2025) for the METR cost data behind the 1% compute bound and either import a feasibility date per process or state the all-feasible assumption. (5) In the abstract, call the ranges "the envelope of our scenario corners" rather than "would reach maximal scores of roughly 40 to 156".

### F2. [Major] The success probability of a transition is sourced from objects it does not measure
- Location: Section 7.3, "Risk" bullet; calibrate_horizons.py lines 143 to 147 and PACE dict; notes/data_sources_part2.md rows project_outcomes.genai_poc_to_production_rate and digital_transformation_success_rate_survey.
- Issue: varpi_p in Definition 4(b) is the probability that a committed migration of one process works (is not reverted). The central value 0.54 is 1 minus the S&P Global share of AI proofs of concept scrapped before production (46%, CIO Dive, 14 March 2025, verified). PoC attrition is a portfolio-selection filter applied before a transition is attempted; it counts exploratory pilots dropped for any reason, and it does not count migrations that reach production and later roll back. The fast value 0.80 is BCG's self-reported share of company-level digital transformations that met their targets when six factors were present (30% overall, 80% with all six; verified). That is a program-level outcome on a 1 to 10 self-assessment, not a per-process embedding probability; the two numbers are not commensurate with each other or with varpi_p. The register itself records the mismatch for the rollback parameter ("Surveys measure abandonment before production, not rollback after cut-over; d") and then uses the PoC rate for varpi anyway. Because q = varpi exp(-nu_D T_emb) multiplies the whole pace, the "23% by 2036 versus all of it by 2033" contrast in the abstract is largely this choice.
- Evidence: CIO Dive: "The average organization scrapped 46% of AI proof-of-concepts before they reached production." BCG (29 Oct 2020): "getting just six things right flips the odds for success from 30% to 80%", success measured by executives' self-assessment of target attainment. Register row transition/rollback_probability_p_fail: "Surveys measure abandonment before production, not rollback after cut-over; d."
- Required change: Either (a) re-source varpi from outcomes of committed system migrations (IT project outcome databases, e.g. Flyvbjerg et al. 2022 on IT cost overruns, ERP cut-over failure rates, or the Bonney et al. 2026 share of adopters who discontinued a function) and state what each measures, or (b) reclassify varpi as a scenario value (class d) and say so in the text in place of "These are survey rates". In both cases cite the sources in the paper (see F11) and report the embedded share's elasticity to varpi.

### F3. [Major] Change capacity (10%, 20% of employees a year) is grounded in layoff rates, which measure separations, not the throughput of organizational change
- Location: Section 7.3, "Capacity" bullet; register rows change_capacity/affected_fte_share_per_year (JOLTS layoffs about 1.0% a month; Alphabet and Meta single restructurings 6 to 13%); calibrate_horizons.py rates(): rho_chg = kappa_abs.
- Issue: The model's Gamma_i for change capacity is the number of affected employees a wave can absorb. A layoff rate counts terminations; a process migration changes the work of many more people than it terminates (the close, support, sales operations), and the voluntary-separations and internal-mobility rates that also absorb change are an order of magnitude larger (JOLTS total separations about 3.2% a month by the register's own note). The paper then maps the share of employees to the share of the 48-process program one for one (rho_chg = kappa_abs), with no basis stated. The consequence is a central pace of 3.0% of the program a year and a completion date of 2060 (calibration_results.json pace.central.complete_year), slower than any observed diffusion of enterprise software (firm AI use went from 3.7% to 18% of firms in 28 months by the paper's own cited sources; Bending Spoons, the paper's own case, restructures "at least with each acquisition"). Since change capacity is the binding constraint in every case (pace.central.binding = "change"), the whole pace rests on this one parameter.
- Required change: Ground kappa_abs in evidence on organizational change throughput (ERP and system-migration rollout shares, the Bonney et al. 2026 growth in functions per adopter, McElheran et al. 2024 adoption dynamics), or label it d; report E_2036 under kappa_abs in {0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.40}; and state the process-to-employee mapping explicitly.

### F4. [Major] The argument that the compute share need not fall as inference prices fall uses the wrong elasticity, and the 1% to 55% bounds measure different objects
- Location: Section 2.4 last paragraph; Section 7.1; Section 7.2 "Taxes, fees and compute"; Table 1 row "Compute price p_c and intensity iota".
- Issue: Demirer et al. (2025) estimate a preliminary short-run price elasticity of demand for LLM tokens just above one across API customers (verified; the abstract calls the estimates preliminary, which the paper omits). Unit elasticity at the market level reflects extensive-margin growth in use: more tasks and more buyers as prices fall. In the model, more tasks automated means lower c_h and a, which is a frontier improvement, not a constant compute share at fixed scope. At the ceiling of Corollary 4 the scope is by definition maximal (c_h = 0, a = a_min + c_full), so a fall in the price of fixed-capability inference lowers c_c = p_c iota / p one for one unless tokens per task rise. Whether they rise is the frontier-tracking question that Gundlach et al. (2025) document (frontier price rising 3 to 18 times a year), which the paper cites for the opposite half of its result only. Separately, the two "credible bounds" on c_c are incommensurable: 1% is the cost of replacing the firm's own labor at METR-observed inference cost (an operating-cost substitution), 55% is the entire 2025 cost of revenue of AI products (which includes hosting, support, third-party and the fees f that Assumption 3 counts separately). Full automation of a conventional software firm's own work at observed cost is near the 1% end; 20% is reached only if the firm's product is itself inference-heavy, which the text says only for the 40% case.
- Required change: Replace the elasticity argument with two labelled paths for c_c (fixed-capability models at falling prices; frontier-tracking models at rising cost per task) and state which the 20% central value assumes. Say that the 55% bound is a cost-of-revenue bound that includes f and non-compute items, so c_c + f <= 0.55. Add "preliminary" to the Demirer et al. elasticity. Cite the METR cost data behind the 1% bound.

### F5. [Major] The AI-failure floor uses human review catch rates for a firm that, by construction, has no human reviewers; review cost and common-mode review failure are missing from c_full
- Location: Lemma 4(c), (d); Table 13 rows "Review-proof error rate", "Misbehavior"; calibrate_horizons.py FC['u_rev'], FC['d_m']; params_reliability.yaml review.catch_rate_ordinary (Panko 1999 spreadsheet inspection), review.catch_rate_hallucination (Si et al. 2024; Dratsch et al. 2023), review.catch_rate_misbehavior_automated_monitor.
- Issue: The ceiling is defined by c_h = 0 and a = a_min + c_full. The review-proof rate u-bar = eps_o (1 - d_o) + eps_h (1 - d_h) uses d_o = 0.65 to 0.85 and d_h = 0.40 to 0.60, which are human catch rates (individual and group spreadsheet inspection; crowdworkers and radiologists facing wrong AI suggestions). At full automation review is AI on AI. Proposition 10 shows, for financial controls, that AI layers sharing a provider share common-mode failures, so stacking them does not drive the miss rate below p^cm; the same logic applies to operational review but c_err ignores it and the common-mode term in Lemma 4(e) covers outages, not correlated review misses. Nor is the cost of review itself anywhere in c_full: if review is human it is c_h or a (contradicting c_h = 0), if it is compute it belongs in c_c. The favorable case (u-bar = 1.1%) therefore combines the best human group-inspection rate with zero human cost. Finally L_err = 0.25 to 1.0 times the unit's production cost is a scenario mean for a loss distribution whose own anchors (params_reliability.yaml agentic.loss_scale_anchors_field) span five orders of magnitude; for customer-facing, legal and financial work the loss per undetected error is routinely 10 to 100 times production cost.
- Required change: Either apply the layered-control logic of Proposition 10 to operational review (catch rate bounded by provider diversity) and charge the review's compute in c_c, or keep a human review floor and put its cost in a_min with the text saying so. Report c_err with L_err up to 10 for the share of automated work that is high-stakes. State in Table 13 that the catch rates are human rates.

### F6. [Major] The free-entry limit assumes costless customer acquisition, contradicting Assumption 2 and the paper's own evidence; "essentially all of a high score must be rent" conflates customer capital with a non-replicable input
- Location: Section 3.8, Assumption 6, Proposition 8(b), (c), the intuition paragraph; Section 7.2 third conclusion ("Reaching 100 would require a rent rho of 92.5 points"); Corollary 3(iv).
- Issue: In the Cournot entry game every entrant sells to the whole market at the going price; there is no customer base to build. In Sections 3.1 to 3.6 growth is bought with sigma at diminishing returns because customers are costly to reach, which is the demand-friction evidence the paper cites (Fitzgerald et al. 2024; Argente et al. 2025; Afrouzi et al. 2023), and Section 5 infers a growth weight of 7 to 13 from that spending, i.e. a large shadow value of the installed base. Under free entry with costly acquisition, zero profit holds on the marginal customer and the installed base earns a rent equal to its capitalized acquisition cost, which automation of code (F to 0) does not remove unless it also removes demand frictions (lambda for entrants). The 7.5-point limit and the 92.5-point "rent on something rivals cannot copy" therefore understate the competitive score of any firm with a customer base, and "distribution" in the list of scarce inputs is doing the work the model should do. The paper is also silent on whether AI lowers rivals' acquisition cost (the register has permissibility.delta_lambda for exactly this), which is the IO channel through which automation could erode customer capital.
- Required change: Add customer capital to the entry benchmark (a costly-acquisition version of Proposition 8, in the spirit of Gourio and Rudanko 2014 or the Argente et al. model) or state explicitly that rho includes the rent on the installed base and give its order of magnitude from the S&M-implied weight. Cite the missing literature (Section 5 below). Revise "essentially all of a high score must be rent" accordingly.

### F7. [Moderate] Pass-through is derived but never used quantitatively; Lemma 3(a) has an undefined symbol
- Location: Lemma 3 (appendix_proofs.tex line 128 and proof lines 440 to 441: "with equality if and only if b = 0"); Section 7.2 "The thresholds hold prices fixed while computing costs fall; if falling costs are passed through to customers, they rise"; lever 4.
- Issue: The pass-through elasticity phi is the natural knob between the two polar cases the paper reports (prices fixed; free entry), yet Table 10 and the text give no number for an intermediate case. Software markets are differentiated with switching costs and seat-based pricing, so phi is well inside (0, 1) and is the first-order channel for how AI cost declines reach gamma. The symbol b in "equality if and only if b = 0" is undefined (it is k_u = 0 per the proof; b is also used for the realized benefit share in Assumption 8).
- Required change: Add a pass-through sensitivity to Section 7.2 (phi in {0, 0.5, 1} applied to a stated decline in compute cost) and show its effect on G*; replace b with k_u in Lemma 3(a).

### F8. [Moderate] The central and conservative accountability floors are residual G&A percentiles, not accountability costs; the bottom-up floor rests on illustrative values
- Location: Section 7.2 "Accountability floor"; Section 8.5; Remark 10; Proposition 10(c).
- Issue: a_min = 3% (central) is the tenth-percentile G&A share of firms above $10 billion; at $10 billion of revenue that is $300 million a year, an order of magnitude above audit fees, Section 404 costs and D&O insurance for firms of that size (the paper's own bottom-up figure is 0.24% at $1 billion and falls with scale). Calling it the "accountability floor" conflates two things: the cost of being accountable (small, mostly fixed) and the residual G&A a lean large firm has not yet automated (a judgment). The band's lower end then depends on the number of in-scope AI applications, which the paper handles well. The illustrative Remark 10 values (N_mm = 24, L_mm = $10 million, p^cm 0.002 and 0.005) have no source, and Proposition 10(c)'s "does not fall with scale" rests on N_mm proportional to S; with materiality thresholds that scale with size, the number of material opportunities need not.
- Required change: Rename the central and conservative values "residual G&A floor" (or "unautomated overhead") and keep "accountability floor" for the bottom-up part; state the N_mm proportionality as an assumption; give at least an order-of-magnitude source for p^cm (e.g. restatement and material-weakness base rates from Audit Analytics, or management-override cases) or label all four values d in the text, as the register does.

### F9. [Moderate] Security floor: the favorable case assumes agentic automation adds no exposure, and the risk cost assumes today's spend is Gordon-Loeb optimal
- Location: Lemma 4(b); Table 13 (xi_exp = 1, 2, 3; L_sec = 4, 2, 1); Remark 9.
- Issue: xi_exp = 1 in the favorable case says a firm that moves from read-only AI to agents with write and transaction rights faces no increase in expected incident loss, while the register's own injection block records undefended injection success of 11 to 48% per attempt (AgentDojo; InjecAgent) and defended 0 to 12%. The risk-cost formula also requires that current spending of 0.69% of revenue (IANS 2024, verified, uncited) be optimal and that L_sec, which sets the residual loss, be known; all three L_sec values are class d. The PQC and compliance lines are small and well handled.
- Required change: Set the favorable xi_exp above 1 (1.5 is defensible from the defended injection rates) or justify 1; cite the IANS survey; cite the injection evidence that motivates xi_exp in the paper; report c_sec under the two outer L_sec values as a sensitivity.

### F10. [Moderate] The horizon calibration holds headroom fixed while scaling the surplus to growth above 100%, contradicting Propositions 3 and 4
- Location: Table 11 notes ("Pi scales the 2025 range ... by mu^(1/(1-alpha))"); calibrate_horizons.py Pi_scaled().
- Issue: Pi is scaled for demand pull and capital intensity with h(s) held at its 2025 level. A firm scoring 134 to 156 in 2028 grows at more than 100% a year and, by Proposition 4, draws down ln(s-bar/s0) fast; by 2031 its h is lower and its Pi smaller than the table's 2031 entry allows. The horizon rows are therefore not a path for one firm but three unrelated cross-sections.
- Required change: Either apply Corollary 1 to cap the number of years the 2028 upper end can persist and say the rows are not a path, or scale h forward using Proposition 15 for the central case.

### F12. [Moderate] Bending Spoons: the arithmetic is correct; two framings are unfair to the firm and one is unfair to the reader
- Location: Abstract; Section 1 ("about 160 as reported"); Section 6 "The company's own split"; Table 9; Section 8.3.
- Issue: (a) Every number in Table 9 reproduces from notes/case_bending_spoons_f1_extract.md and the Q2 2026 6-K (verified at SEC): growth 73.4%, 94.7%, 132.2%; margins 19.0%, 21.3%, 20.0%; interest 4.9%, 10.9%, 15.5%; annuity scores 80.9 to 86.8 and 84.4 to 92.8 at CRF 14.6% and 10.8%; fully charged -28.7, -24.8, -129.3; price-to-revenue 2.30, 1.73, 3.23; Q2 2026 scores 160 and 37. (b) "about 160 as reported" (abstract, Section 1): the company reports no Rule-of-40 score; 160 is the authors' sum of reported growth and margin. Say "on reported growth". (c) The "organic score of about 37" pairs organic growth of 3% with a Q2 2026 GAAP margin that carries $50.8 million of reorganization expense (7.2% of revenue) from integrating acquisitions and ignores $109 million of interest (15.5%) on acquisition debt. The organic score is thus about 21 after the paper's own interest charge and about 44 before the acquisition-driven reorganization charge; the paper should show the range rather than a single 37, since Section 6 argues that both items are acquisition costs. (d) "absent new customers, standing still requires acquiring revenue at that rate" (abstract, Section 1): the company's Q2 2026 organic growth of +3% already nets the NRR shortfall against new customers (79 to 83% of new-customer revenue from organic channels per the F-1), so the treadmill is a hypothetical, not a description. (e) The paper applies to Bending Spoons the quarterly year-over-year growth plus quarterly margin construction it declares non-comparable for Palantir (Section 4.5); it says so, but the abstract does not.
- Required change: Reword (b) and (d); add the two alternative organic scores in (c); add "quarterly, on the construction Section 4.5 rejects for annual comparison" at the first mention. The annuity life and the net-investing-outflow proxy are labelled scenario and lower bound, which is fair.

### F13. [Moderate] The AI mapping (Table 1, Proposition 9) and the G scenario do not net the channels the paper itself lists
- Location: Table 1; Section 7.1; lever 4 ("customers' headcount, and with it their seat counts, shrinks"); lever 6; HZ G bands.
- Issue: The paper says AI raises G where labor budgets shift to software and lowers it where seats shrink, and that AI can raise lambda for incumbents; it does not say AI can raise lambda for entrants (eroding incumbents' h) or lower nominal G through price deflation (its own Lemma 3(b)). The G bands then run to 30% in 2036 without netting any of this. An IO reader will also note that the paper's evidence in Section 5 (fast growers scarcer, median growth 12%) points the other way.
- Required change: State the sign of each channel in Table 1 for incumbents and entrants, and tie the G bands to a stated decomposition (software's share of labor budgets, seat compression, price deflation).

### F14. [Moderate] Disruption and realization parameters remove 66% of attempts in the central case and are mostly class d
- Location: Section 7.3 "Risk" and "Embedding"; calibration_results.json pace.central (q = 0.34, nu_D = 0.232).
- Issue: nu_D = 0.23 a year with T_emb = 2 years discards 37% of otherwise successful transitions to leadership and strategy change; nu_V = 0.15 and p_keep = 0.60 are class d, and a reverted attempt retains nothing (no partial learning, no reusable build), although the register has a reuse-fraction block. Combined with F2 and F3 this is why the central program completes in 2060.
- Required change: Report E_2036 under nu_D in {0.07, 0.15, 0.23} and with partial retention of reverted builds; say that the central case is at the pessimistic end of the paper's own register ranges, or move it toward the middle.

## 5. Missing literature, 2018 to 2026 (each verified at a publisher, NBER, RePEc, arXiv or issuer page on 29 October 2026)

1. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018), "The race between man and machine," AER 108(6): 1488-1542. The canonical task-based automation framework that Section 2.4 invokes ("task-based models of automation") without citing; the displacement-versus-new-tasks distinction maps directly onto a, c_h and G.
2. Jones and Tonetti (2020), "Nonrivalry and the economics of data," AER 110(9): 2819-2858. Canonical for data as a non-rival, partly excludable input; needed where Section 3.8 and lever 1 treat data as the scarce input supporting rho.
3. Goldfarb and Tucker (2019), "Digital economics," JEL 57(1): 3-43. The survey of why digital goods have near-zero marginal cost and where rents come from (search, replication, transport, tracking, verification costs); the natural frame for the pass-through and free-entry discussion.
4. Korinek and Vipra (2025), "Concentrating intelligence: scaling and market structure in artificial intelligence," Economic Policy 40(121): 225-256 (NBER 33139). Directly on the IO of the AI supply chain and provider concentration, which the common-mode term of Lemma 4(e) and Section 2.5's "rising fixed cost" paragraph assume without a reference.
5. Kwa et al. (2025), "Measuring AI ability to complete long software tasks," arXiv:2503.14499 (METR). The source of the task-horizon series and of the per-run cost data behind the 1% compute bound; needed for F1 and F4.
6. McElheran, Li, Brynjolfsson, Kroff, Dinlersoz, Foster, Zolas (2024), "AI adoption in America: who, what, and where," Journal of Economics and Management Strategy 33(2): 375-415 (NBER 31788). The firm-level adoption evidence from the Annual Business Survey; relevant to F3 (change throughput) and Section 2.4.
7. Ide and Talamàs (2025), "Artificial intelligence in the knowledge economy," Journal of Political Economy (doi 10.1086/737233). A model of how AI changes the hierarchy and span of the firm; bears on the accountability floor (who signs) and the residual organization.
8. Humlum and Vestergaard (2025), "Large language models, small labor market effects," NBER 33777. Danish administrative evidence that realized time savings and labor effects are small; relevant to the realized-benefit share b-bar and the central pace.
9. Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen (2025), "Canaries in the coal mine? Six facts about the recent employment effects of artificial intelligence," Stanford Digital Economy Lab working paper (Nov 2025 version). Early-career employment declines in exposed occupations; relevant to the seat-compression channel in lever 4 and to G.
10. Noy and Zhang (2023), "Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence," Science 381(6654): 187-192. With Brynjolfsson et al. (2025), the core experimental productivity evidence behind c_h and a.
11. Dell'Acqua, McFowland, Mollick et al. (2023), "Navigating the jagged technological frontier," HBS Working Paper 24-013. Quality falls when consultants use AI outside the frontier: the field evidence for the error and automation-bias parameters in Lemma 4(c).
12. Korinek and Suh (2024), "Scenarios for the transition to AGI," NBER 32255. Scenario construction for horizons under capability growth; the methodological reference the paper's Section 7.3 lacks.
13. Weyl and Fabinger (2013), "Pass-through as an economic tool," JPE 121(3): 528-583. Pre-2018 but canonical; the general pass-through formulas that Lemma 3(a) special-cases, including the conduct-parameter form that would let the paper interpolate between its two polar cases.
14. Ge, Koester and McVay (2017), "Benefits and costs of Sarbanes-Oxley Section 404(b) exemption," JAE 63(2-3): 358-384. Evidence on the dollar cost of 404(b) attestation; grounds the bottom-up accountability floor and the emerging-growth-company exemption discussed in Section 8.5 (edge of the 2018 window, but the standard reference).

Suggested but not essential: Gourio and Rudanko (2014, REStud) on customer capital for F6; Autor, Chin, Salomons and Seegmiller (2024, QJE) on new tasks for G; Flyvbjerg et al. (2022, JMIS) on IT project outcomes for F2.
